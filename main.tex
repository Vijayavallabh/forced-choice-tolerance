\begin{abstract}
BixBench grades an agent's free-text answer open-ended and through multiple choice: a second model reads the agent's
notebook and answer and selects one of four options, forced to choose or allowed to refuse. The forced-choice score is
still reported in 2026. On the benchmark's numeric questions we compare both gradings with a tolerance (is the
submitted number within $5\%$ of the key?) on its published runs, on open-weight agents and on three current agents.
Forced to choose, BixBench's grader accepts a fifth to a quarter of the wrong answers, about as many as a random
choice among four would, but it favours the misses whose nearest option is the key: on the published runs it accepts
them $3.5$ and $6.0$ times as often as those nearest a distractor. The classical correction for guessing assumes a
random choice, so it agrees with the tolerance on the published runs only because these errors cancel; on the current
release it falls below the tolerance, largely because it scores a grader's non-selections as wrong guesses. On current
agents forced choice still adds $10$ to $12$ points. The options also leak the key: v1.5 places it second-smallest on
$51\%$ of numeric items, a rank that a rule ignoring the question learns on held-out capsules. For a grader that
follows the nearest option, hiding the rank conflicts with rejecting misses, because a uniformly drawn rank leaves
keys at an extreme, where every miss beyond the key and of its sign is nearest it. The cost is large for graders that
see the answer and the options but not the notebook and smaller for graders given BixBench's prompt and the notebook,
although BixBench's published Claude 3.5 Sonnet grades follow the nearest option as closely as the former. Numeric
answers should be graded by tolerance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

BixBench \citep{bixbench} asks an agent to analyse a biological dataset in a notebook and to submit a free-text
answer. The answer is graded open-ended and also through multiple choice: a second model, the \emph{MCQ grader},
receives the notebook, the question with four options and the answer, and selects the best option from the notebook,
with the answer ``as a reference'', either \emph{forced} to choose or allowed to \emph{refuse}. BixBench's authors
treat the open answer as primary and the options as
``a useful proxy'': multiple-choice scores are ``marginally better than random'', and they ``speculate'' that
removing the refusal option raises them because a model is ``answering via recall rather than the information
contained in the analysis''. The forced-choice score remains in use: a 2026 system reports $64.4\%$ forced-choice
against $48.8\%$ open-ended accuracy on v1.5, graded by a model that sees the answer and the options but not the
notebook, and sets it beside published forced-choice scores graded with the notebook \citep{bioagents}. What such a
score consists of has not been measured.

We measure it where the reference needs no grader. On the questions whose options are numbers ($159$ in v1.0 and
$105$ in v1.5), a \emph{tolerance} accepts a submitted number within $5\%$ of the key, as SciBench's grading does
\citep{scibench}; on BixBench's published answers it disagrees with the benchmark's own open-ended grader on $2.3\%$
of verdicts (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}). Because the agent never sees the options, the same answers can be regraded
through any option set after the run. We regrade BixBench's published runs, our runs of open-weight agents on v1.5
and runs of three current agents (gpt-5.1, gpt-6-luna and DeepSeek-V4-Pro), and find three things:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\topsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
\item \textbf{Forced choice accepts misses by where they fall among the options} (\S\ref{sec:withdata}). BixBench's
grader accepts $22.6$ and $25.3\%$ of the misses on the published gpt-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet runs, near a random
choice's $25\%$, but $44.0$ and $68.3\%$ of those whose nearest option is the key and $12.7$ and $11.4\%$ of those
nearest another. gpt-4o's rate is lowered by the $24.9\%$ of misses on which its reply selects no option, which
BixBench's parser scores wrong. Of the $19.2$ and $21.0$ points forced choice adds over the tolerance, the misses
nearest the key contribute $4.5$ and $9.3$; the rest comes from misses the options place elsewhere and from answers
that give no number. Current agents are still scored $10.0$ to $11.9$ points above the tolerance, although
gpt-6-luna's interval reaches $-1.1$ when the paper's ten headline findings are corrected jointly
(Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).
\item \textbf{The correction for guessing agrees with the tolerance only by cancellation} (\S\ref{sec:withdata}).
Formula scoring removes what a random choice would add. It lands $3.9$ and $0.1$ points below the tolerance on the
published runs as a sum of opposite parts, and $6.0$ to $10.8$ points below it on v1.5, largely because it scores a
grader's non-selections as wrong guesses. Scored as omissions, the seven configurations' own gradings sit $3.0$ points
below the tolerance and the current agents' $1.7$ to $5.4$ above.
\item \textbf{v1.5's options leak the key's rank} (\S\ref{sec:theory}). The key is second-smallest on $51.4\%$ of
v1.5's $105$ numeric items, worth $+26.4$ points to a rule whose rank is chosen on held-out capsules; the skew
is sharpest in the items whose distractors v1.5 rewrote.
\end{itemize}
For a grader that selects the option nearest the submitted number, hiding the key's rank conflicts with rejecting
misses (Observation~\ref{obs:bracket}, Figure~\ref{fig:overview}a). Distractors on both sides of the key reject misses
in both directions but give the key a middle rank; a rank drawn uniformly leaves $2/k$ of the keys at an extreme in
expectation, where every miss of the key's sign on the side without a distractor is nearest the key. The observation
is elementary; we measure its price for real graders (\S\ref{sec:cost}, Table~\ref{tab:cost}). We regrade the same
runs through a rewrite that draws the key's rank uniformly ($U$) and one that keeps it ($P$). The nearest-option rule
accepts $16.7$ points more on the v1.5 keys that $U$ moves to an extreme, and $5.3$ more over all numeric items.
Code-free graders gain about as much or more. Graders given BixBench's prompt and the notebook gain $5.9$ to $14.4$
points on the published runs' moved keys and at most $3.0$ over all items, $0.7$ to $6.8$ on new runs' moved keys
(none significant by a capsule sign-flip test) and $-2.7$ to $+4.1$ on the current agents'. BixBench's own
published grades cannot be regraded, but how often they select the nearest option predicts $+6.9$ on the moved keys
for gpt-4o's and $+20.1$ for Claude 3.5 Sonnet's. An option stating that no value is within the tolerance removes the
conflict, as the tolerance itself does. We do not show that any model uses the rank, so the leak threatens
BixBench's no-data baseline, not its scores with the data, and we show no published ranking of agents that forced
choice reverses.

\paragraph{Terms.} A \emph{capsule} is a BixBench dataset with its questions; every interval resamples capsules. A
\emph{miss} is an answer whose submitted number, the last number in the answer, is more than $5\%$ from the key, or
that gives none. A \emph{code-free} grader is shown the question, the options and the answer but not the notebook,
approximating the 2026 system's grader, and asked for the option that matches the answer (prompts in
Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The \emph{nearest-option rule} selects the option nearest the submitted
number, with no model; as the pre-specified test fixed it, it reads a number only when the whole answer is one and
scores any other answer $0$, which understates its gains (Table~\ref{tab:lastnumber}). A key is \emph{bracketed} if
distractors lie on both sides of it and \emph{extreme} otherwise;
the side of an extreme key without a distractor is its \emph{open side}. $R$ are the released options; one generator
(Appendix~\ref{app:withdata}) builds $P$, which redraws every distractor and keeps the key's rank, and $U$, which
draws the rank uniformly and so moves some keys from bracketed to extreme (the \emph{moved keys}). A
\emph{configuration} is one agent model under one protocol. Every interval in the main text is a percentile bootstrap
over capsules at the nominal level that attains $95\%$ coverage on the group's own capsules, and none is given where no
level does (Appendix~\ref{app:stats});
Table~\ref{tab:glossary} lists every term.


\section{What forced-choice grading accepts}
\label{sec:withdata}

We grade three sets of with-data runs on the numeric questions (Appendix~\ref{app:withdata}). BixBench's
\emph{published runs}, released with the benchmark, are $5{,}161$ runs of gpt-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet on v1.0's
$159$ numeric questions, each graded by the model that made it. On v1.5 we ran BixBench's agent, with and without
each capsule's data, for five open-weight families under two protocols: tools called in text (Qwen2.5-72B,
Llama-3.3-70B, gemma-3-27b) and BixBench's published ReAct agent (Qwen2.5-72B, Llama-3.3-70B, GLM-4.5-Air,
Qwen3-30B-A3B). Each configuration is graded by its own model, as the published runs were (Qwen3-30B-A3B's by
gemma-3-27b), and by one grader for all, gemma-3-27b. Later we added two seeds of each text-protocol agent and two
runs of Qwen3-235B-A22B. Three current agents, gpt-5.1, gpt-6-luna and DeepSeek-V4-Pro, ran BixBench's published
agent and were graded by gpt-4o (version 2024-11-20), one of BixBench's two graders.

\paragraph{The released options.} Forced to choose, BixBench's grader accepts $22.6$ and $25.3\%$ of the misses it
grades on the published runs, where a random choice among four would accept $25\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:released}). Which
misses it accepts is not random. It accepts $44.0$ and $68.3\%$ of the misses whose nearest option is the key (v1.0
leaves $39\%$ of its keys extreme) and $12.7$ and $11.4\%$ of those nearest another option. gpt-4o's grades select no
option on $24.9\%$ of its misses, which BixBench's parser scores wrong, and accept $30.1\%$ of the others, more than a
random choice would. The grader rejects $2.9$ and $1.6\%$ of the correct answers, and so scores the published runs
$+19.2$
$[+15.5,+23.4]$ and $+21.0$ $[+17.3,+25.0]$ points above the tolerance. The excess splits exactly by what the answer
gives: the misses nearest the key contribute $4.5$ and $9.3$ points, those nearest another option $5.1$ and $5.8$,
and the answers with no number or no single nearest option $9.9$ and $6.2$. The grader accepts $29$ and $34\%$ of
the answers with no number, although BixBench's open-ended graders accept almost none of these and the key's value is
in the notebook for only $16$ and $7\%$ of them. Most of what forced choice adds is thus a choice on answers the
options do not place near the key; the options decide how much more the misses near the key receive. The other runs
show the same excess. The seven v1.5 configurations are scored $+14.6$ $[+12.0,+17.2]$ points above the tolerance by
their own models and $+15.2$ $[+11.2,+19.0]$ by gemma-3-27b for all seven, so the excess does not come from
self-grading; the current agents are scored $+10.0$ to $+11.9$, of which the misses nearest the key contribute $2.6$
to $4.3$. With a refusal option, BixBench's grading accepts $2.0$ and $5.6\%$ of the published runs' misses but
rejects $8.8$ and $10.2\%$ of the correct answers, refusing $82$ and $69\%$ of runs, and lands $+0.7$ and $+3.1$
points from the tolerance, the net of the misses it accepts and the correct answers it rejects.

The excess does not come from how the tolerance reads an answer. Against BixBench's own open-ended grading of the same
runs it is $+20.4$ and $+21.7$. At every tolerance from $1$ to $10\%$ it is $13$ to $24$ points on the published runs
and the seven configurations, and $7$ to $18$ on the other runs graded with the data (Table~\ref{tab:restated}).
Grading the $19$ keys that are p-values within a factor of ten leaves $+17.5$ and $+19.1$ (Table~\ref{tab:reference}).
The excess is not confined to numbers: on the questions whose options are not numbers, the published forced grades
exceed the open-ended ones by $+29.0$ and $+20.2$ points, against $+21.9$ and $+19.4$ at chance (Table~\ref{tab:allq}).
Nor does it depend on the notebook. Graded code-free, as the 2026 system grades, the published runs score $+27.2$ and
$+21.6$ points above BixBench's open-ended grades over all questions, where its forced grades with the notebook score
$+24.3$ and $+21.0$; gpt-5.1's runs over all $205$ v1.5 questions score $+21.7$ code-free against $+15.2$ with the
notebook. Forced-choice scores taken by different graders are therefore not comparable.

\paragraph{Corrected for guessing.} Formula scoring, the classical correction for guessing
\citep{lordformula,frary}, maps a forced score $S$ over $k$ options to $(S-1/k)/(1-1/k)$, which leaves a grader that
accepts every correct answer and chooses at random on every miss at the tolerance. It brings the published runs to
$-3.9$ $[-8.6,+1.3]$ and $-0.1$ $[-4.7,+4.7]$ points from the tolerance, but as a sum of opposite parts
(Table~\ref{tab:formula}): beyond a random choice, the misses nearest the key add $2.6$ and $7.8$ points and those
nearest another option subtract $6.5$ and $9.3$. On v1.5, whose options bracket $81\%$ of the keys and whose graders
reject more correct answers, the same correction lands $6.0$ to $10.8$ points below the tolerance, the current agents
included. Much of that gap comes from how it counts the parser's failures. Scoring a grade that selects no option as
an omission, as formula scoring treats an examinee who omits, moves gpt-4o's published residual from $-3.9$ to
$+4.0$, the seven configurations' from $-10.6$ to $-3.0$ and the current agents' to $+1.7$ to $+5.4$
(Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). The correction maps the 2026 system's $64.4\%$ to $52.5\%$, $3.7$ points above its
open-ended $48.8\%$. How far that lies from a tolerance depends on where its misses fall among the options, which the
correction does not see. Being monotone, the correction also leaves every ranking as forced choice made it.

\section{What the options reveal}
\label{sec:theory}

Sort an item's $k$ numeric values and let $p_j$ be the share of items whose key has rank $j$; a rule that always
selects rank $j$, ignoring the question, scores $p_j$. On BixBench v1.5's $105$ four-option numeric items ($46$
capsules) the key's rank distribution is $(p_1,\dots,p_4)=(12.4,51.4,29.5,6.7)\%$, far from uniform with items
clustered by capsule ($p=4\times10^{-8}$; Figure~\ref{fig:overview}b). The second-smallest rule scores $+26.4$
$[+14.0,+38.6]$ points above chance, correct on $54$ of the $105$, and choosing the rank out of sample changes
nothing: in each of $46$ leave-one-capsule-out folds the rank chosen on the other $45$ capsules is the
second-smallest. On v1.0's $159$ numeric items the best rule's in-sample $+5.8$ falls to $-2.4$ when its rank is
chosen on held-out folds. The skew arose in v1.5 (Table~\ref{tab:origin}): in the $24$ of v1.0's items whose
distractors it rewrote, the key is second-smallest on $19$ ($79\%$, up from $29\%$ in v1.0), with new distractors
typically $0.59$, $1.40$ and $1.78$ times the key; its $43$ new items place the key there on $40\%$. We do not show
that any model we tested uses the rank, with or without solved examples in context (Appendix~\ref{app:arms}). The rank
threatens BixBench's no-data baseline, which grades a model's choice among the options without the data.

\paragraph{Hiding the rank and rejecting misses.} Let the nearest-option rule select, for a submitted number $a$, the
option $v$ that minimises $d(a,v)=|a-v|/(|a|+|v|)$. For values of one sign $d=\tanh(|\log|a|-\log|v||/2)$, so the
nearest option is the nearest on a log scale, the scale of Figure~\ref{fig:overview}a, and an option of the other sign
is at distance $1$.

\begin{observation}[the nearest-option rule]
\label{obs:bracket}
Let a key $y\neq0$ have its nearest options of its own sign at factors $\rho_-$ below and $\rho_+$ above it in
magnitude. The rule accepts exactly the misses of $y$'s sign within factors $\sqrt{\rho_-}$ and $\sqrt{\rho_+}$ of
it, and a key with no such option on one side (an extreme key) also every miss beyond it on that side, however large.
Hence, among $k$ options, a key rank drawn uniformly leaves $2/k$ of the keys extreme in expectation, and a rank kept
off the extremes lets a rule that ignores the question gain at least $1/(k-2)-1/k=2/(k(k-2))$ over chance.
\end{observation}

The observation is elementary (one-dimensional nearest-neighbour cells with boundaries at geometric midpoints;
Appendix~\ref{app:theory}) and binds only a grader that follows the nearest option. It prices each design: a rank kept
off the extremes leaks at least $25$ points at $k=4$ and $4.2$ at $k=8$, a uniform rank opens one side of $2/k$ of the
keys, and closer spacing narrows what a bracketed key accepts. A tolerance rejects any miss larger than $t$ whatever
the options, so it is outside the conflict, and so is a grader offered an option saying that no value is within $t$.
Section~\ref{sec:cost} measures how far real graders follow the nearest option.

\section{What hiding the rank costs}
\label{sec:cost}

The agent never sees the options, so they can be replaced after a run. $U$ moves $37$ of v1.5's $105$ numeric keys
from bracketed to extreme and $12$ from extreme to bracketed (the \emph{inward keys}), and leaves $56$ in their class
(the \emph{unchanged keys}); $P$ moves none. On the moved keys $U-P$ isolates the key's rank, with the redrawing of
the distractors held fixed; over all items it is what a benchmark would pay for drawing the rank uniformly, since $U$
also moves keys inward, where the rule and most gradings lose. Table~\ref{tab:cost} gives both for every grader and
set of runs.

Under the nearest-option rule, hiding the rank raises the share of the seven configurations' answers graded correct
by $+16.7$ $[+10.2,+23.0]$ points on the moved keys and by $+5.3$ $[+1.9,+8.6]$ over all numeric items. Of the $47$
answers that $U$ newly accepts with the data, $46$ lie on the key's open side and $16$ are off by more than $100\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}). Code-free graders follow the nearest option most closely and bear most of the cost,
$+15.3$ to $+28.2$ points on the moved keys of the published runs, the seven configurations and the new seeds. Graders
given BixBench's prompt and the notebook bear less: $+5.9$ to $+14.4$ on the published runs' moved keys and $+0.5$ to
$+3.0$ over all their items, against the rule's $+3.4$. On the new seeds they bear $+0.7$ to $+6.8$, none significant
by a capsule sign-flip test ($p=0.059$ to $0.861$), and on the current agents gpt-4o's change is $-2.7$ to $+4.1$.
BixBench's own published grades exist only through $R$, but how often they select the nearest option predicts what
they would bear on the published runs' moved keys: $+6.9$ for gpt-4o's grades, whose proximity weight is $0.24$, and
$+20.1$ for Claude 3.5 Sonnet's, whose $0.58$ lies within the code-free graders' $0.48$ to $0.69$
(Remark~\ref{rem:lambda}, Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). With an option stating that none of the others is within
$5\%$ of the answer, code-free graders change by $-2.0$ to $-0.1$ points: the option turns grading through options
into a tolerance.

The cost falls on misses, so it shrinks as agents improve. Over the nineteen configurations with the data, the rule's

generator adds; they move the rule's contrasts on the moved and unchanged keys and over all items by at most $2.9$
points, except DeepSeek-V4-Pro's, which rest on few accepted answers (Appendix~\ref{app:withdata}).

\paragraph{The test fixed in advance.} Before analysing runs that no earlier analysis had used (the published runs,
and new v1.5 runs made afterwards), we fixed six hypotheses about the nearest-option rule's contrasts, each with a
pass criterion, in a dated plan that was not deposited publicly (Appendix~\ref{app:replication},
Table~\ref{tab:predictions}). Observation~\ref{obs:bracket} fixes the direction of three of them (the moved keys
gain, the inward keys do not, and the newly accepted answers are far misses on the open side), so these test only
magnitude; they pass on the published runs and the new seeds. The moved keys gain $+19.8$ $[+10.4,+31.2]$ points
under $U$ against $R$ on the published runs and $+17.6$ $[+9.0,+25.8]$ on the new seeds; for Qwen3-235B-A22B the
moved keys' $+9.5$ $[+0.0,+22.1]$ reaches zero and H1 fails. The two informative hypotheses, that the unchanged keys
do not change (H2) and that the gain is not the redrawing's (H4), each failed on some runs. H4 holds on the published
runs and the new seeds, whose moved keys gain $+18.6$ and $+17.9$ points against $P$. On the published runs the
unchanged keys' $+3.7$ $[+0.9,+6.9]$ excludes zero, and $2.4$ points of it come from $11$ keys that $U$ moves from one
extreme to the other; we count H2 as failed there, although its stated criterion, which also accepts a mean within
$5$ points of zero, passes it. Qwen3-235B-A22B, whose number is within $5\%$ of the key on $23.8\%$ of numeric
questions, fails both: its unchanged keys gain $+8.9$ $[+2.7,+16.3]$ and its moved keys $+4.7$ $[-0.8,+9.7]$ against
$P$. Only these contrasts of the rule were fixed in advance; the split into moved and unchanged keys was chosen after
the first v1.5 results, and every MCQ grading is exploratory. Read from the last number in the answer, as the
tolerance reads it, every hypothesis keeps its verdict on every data set, and the moved keys gain more against $P$:
$+21.9$ and $+24.2$ on the published runs and the new seeds (Table~\ref{tab:lastnumber}).

\paragraph{Rankings.} On BixBench's published runs Claude 3.5 Sonnet leads gpt-4o under every grading. On the seven
v1.5 configurations, the tolerance preserves their open-answer ranking better than each configuration's own MCQ
grader, by $+0.85$ in Kendall's $\tau$, and better than gemma-3-27b as one grader for all, by $+0.60$, but both
intervals reach zero (Table~\ref{tab:ranking}). Seven configurations cannot settle this, nor separate grading from
self-grading, and we show no ranking that forced choice reverses.

\section{Other designs}
\label{sec:design}

Observation~\ref{obs:bracket} prices the conflict in the number and spacing of the options. We rebuilt every numeric
item's distractors with the generator of $P$ and $U$ under designs an author could choose, and regraded the same runs
by the rule, reading the last number in the answer, and by the four code-free graders (Table~\ref{tab:design};
Appendix~\ref{app:design}). Distractors taken from agents' errors leave the cost in place: drawing their key's rank
uniformly rather than at its released rank raises the rule's acceptance on the keys it moves to an extreme by $+24.1$
$[+16.3,+33.8]$ on the published runs and $+18.8$ $[+11.3,+28.1]$ on v1.5, as the generator's distractors do ($+24.9$
and $+18.1$). More options shrink both sides of the conflict. Each moved key gains $16$ to $26$ points at every $k$,
but only $2/k$ of the keys move, so a uniform rank costs the rule $11.1$ and $8.4$ points over all items against one
kept off the extremes at $k=4$ on the published runs and v1.5, and $6.1$ and $4.5$ at $k=8$
(Table~\ref{tab:designpairs}). The rank kept off the extremes leaks at least $25$ and $4.2$ points in theory, and
$+30.1$ and $+9.0$ on held-out capsules of v1.0. Closer spacing narrows what a bracketed key accepts: with eight
options a fifth of the key apart and the key never extreme, code-free graders accept $10.5$ to $13.0\%$ of misses
(against $20.2$ to $33.6\%$ through four options redrawn at the released ranks) and $92.0$ to $100.0\%$ of correct
answers. No design removes both costs for a grader that follows the nearest option. The within-$5\%$ option does, by
grading the tolerance itself, at the price of rejecting $6.6$ to $17.9\%$ of correct answers.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\emph{Grading free answers.} Matching an answer against a reference agrees with human grading where multiple choice
does not \citep{answermatching}, and open-ended questions remove the options' cues at the cost of a grader
\citep{openstyle}; for a number the reference check is a tolerance, SciBench's $5\%$ \citep{scibench}. Verifiers that
decide whether a final answer matches a reference, rule-based or trained models such as xVerify and CompassVerifier
\citep{xverify,compassverifier}, fail in two ways: rules miss answers written in another form, and model judges can
be led to accept wrong ones \citep{verifierpitfalls}. Our tolerance is a rule and inherits the first failure, which is
why we check its extraction; forced choice is a third kind of verifier, a judge that must map the answer to a listed
value. An MCQ grader is a model judging another model's output, and such judges are swayed by position, length and
their own style \citep{llmjudge,fairevaluators} and lean toward leniency \citep{judgingjudges}; forced choice builds a
leniency into the protocol, and formula scoring \citep{lordformula,frary}, which assumes that a guess falls on the
options at random, cannot remove it. \emph{Choices without the question.} BixBench's no-data baseline is the
hypothesis-only baseline of natural language inference \citep{hypothesisonly,artifacts} applied to multiple choice,
where models answer from the choices alone \citep{choicesonly}; such cues are shortcuts
\citep{shortcut,rightwrong,niven}.
``Choose the middle value'' is a test-wiseness cue identified sixty years ago \citep{testwiseness}, and test writers
place keys in middle \emph{positions} \citep{attalibarhillel}, which shuffling removes
\citep{selectionbias,positionsensitivity}; the middle \emph{value} survives it. Plausible errors are the item-writing
prescription for distractors \citep{haladynataxonomy,haladynarodriguez} and the target of automated generators
\citep{distractorgeneration}; that literature asks whether a distractor functions for examinees
\citep{distractorfunction} and how many options an item needs \citep{howmanyoptions,threeoptions}, not what the options
reveal without the question. Contamination \citep{memorisationexploitation,contaminationsurvey,taskcontamination} is
tested on the \emph{model}, through its likelihoods \citep{minkprob}, completions
\citep{memorisation,timetravel,tsguessing} or preference for a benchmark's canonical order
\citep{exchangeablecontamination}; we examine the benchmark. \emph{Agent benchmarks.} Agent benchmarks have been
criticised for missing holdout sets \citep{agentsmatter} and for reward designs that over- or underestimate performance
\citep{abc}; the rank cue is such a shortcut and forced-choice grading such a reward design. A concurrent audit of
BixBench \citep{bixbenchaudit} re-derives the answer keys of three capsules ($14$ questions) from the raw data and
grades $140$ replicated agent runs on them, taking the options as given. BixBench-Verified-50 re-checks fifty of its
questions with domain experts and grades the open answer, $13$ by a numeric range, keeping the distractors
\citep{verified50}; frontier agents with added skills now answer $84$ to $98\%$ of them \citep{zhang2026}. The agent
benchmarks for scientific data analysis that we know of grade without options
\citep{corebench,discoverybench,scienceagentbench}, or, as LAB-Bench's SeqQA does, show the numeric options to the
model that answers \citep{labbench}; BixBench is the one we know of that grades free-text numeric answers through
options, and option-free grading is the design we recommend.

\section{Recommendations and limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\emph{Grade numeric answers by tolerance, on a number extracted the same way for every grading.} Which misses forced
choice accepts depends on how the options were written; a tolerance asks how far the number is from the key. Our $5\%$,
also SciBench's, agrees with BixBench's own judgment: the $60$ keys its authors wrote as ranges have a median
half-width of $6.1\%$, and over $5{,}161$ of gpt-4o's and Claude 3.5 Sonnet's published answers the v1.0 grader and a
$5\%$ rule disagree on $120$ ($2.3\%$; $\kappa=0.90$, the highest of the rules we tried). We apply an absolute
tolerance $t$ to keys of zero and a relative one to all others. A p-value may warrant a tolerance on the log scale,
which leaves the forced-choice excess in place but not every comparison between agents
(Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). \emph{Report how many answers contain no number}: more than a quarter of gpt-4o's
published answers to numeric questions contain none, and forced-choice grading accepts $29\%$ of those. \emph{Check
the extraction against the answers}: one current agent states its number first and discusses others after it, which
defeats a rule that reads the last number (Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). \emph{Do not rely on a correction for
guessing}: it recovers the tolerance on BixBench's published runs only because two parts that depend on the options
cancel, lands $6$ to $11$ points below it on v1.5, and cannot change a ranking. \emph{Do not compare forced-choice
scores taken by different graders}: graded without the notebook, the same runs score $0.6$ to $6.5$ points more over
all questions. \emph{If options must grade free answers, add an option saying that no value is within the
tolerance}, and report the correct answers it rejects: code-free graders reject $6.6$ to $17.9\%$ with it, and
BixBench's refusal option $8.8$ and $10.2\%$. Where a grader cannot be given such an option, use more options closer
to the key: eight a fifth of the key apart leave code-free graders accepting $10.5$ to $13.0\%$ of misses. A key kept
off the extremes then leaks its rank ($+13.4$ and $+10.6$ points on held-out capsules), so such options suit grading,
not a no-data baseline. \emph{Where a model answers from the options without the data}, as in BixBench's no-data
baseline, draw the rank uniformly, publish the key-rank histogram and write distractors as round as those they replace
(Appendix~\ref{app:withdata}).

\paragraph{Limitations.} The reference is checked against BixBench's own graders, not against human judgment: no
human audited the accepted misses or the tolerance's verdicts, which were checked against the notebooks and for
scaling errors automatically. On v1.5, tolerances of $1$ and $2\%$ agree with BixBench's graders better than $5\%$
($\kappa=0.81$ against $0.77$). Every tolerance-dependent result is restated at $1$, $2$ and $10\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:restated}): the forced-choice excess stays positive at each, though at $10\%$ the intervals of gpt-5.1
and gpt-6-luna reach zero; the refusal option's small excess excludes zero at $1$ and $2\%$ on the published runs and
the seven configurations; and Qwen2.5-72B's lead over Llama-3.3-70B is significant only at $5\%$. A fourth current
agent, Kimi-K3, is left out because its long answers state the number first: gpt-4o grades $53.7\%$ of them correct
open-ended, while the last number in the answer is within $5\%$ of the key in $32.6\%$
(Appendix~\ref{app:replication}).

Our open-weight agents, run at temperature $1.0$, are weak: with the data their number is within $5\%$ of the key on
$2$ to $24\%$ of numeric items, only Qwen2.5-72B among the text-protocol models measurably uses the data, and both
Qwen families loop in the published protocol's reasoning step. The three current agents reach $28$ to $38\%$ and are
still scored $+10.0$ to $+11.9$ points above the tolerance forced (Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). Leaving out the
$123$ of the seven configurations' $735$ runs that gave no answer, reached the step limit or had a reply cut at the
token limit raises their forced-choice excess from $+14.6$ to $+17.3$ and the rule's moved-key gain from $+16.7$ to
$+18.8$ $[+11.7,+25.9]$. What forced choice adds scales with the misses, so it shrinks as agents improve, but not per
miss. Over the $22$ configurations with the data, the share of misses their forced graders accept does not fall as
agents land within $5\%$ of the key more often (Spearman $\rho=+0.07$, $p=0.75$), and the share of misses nearest the
key rises ($\rho=+0.66$, $p=0.001$).

Where the original graders cannot be run, we substitute. gpt-4o 2024-11-20 replaces 2024-08-06, which Azure OpenAI,
where our runs are served, no longer offers to new customers; 2024-08-06 selected no option on $24.4\%$ of the
published runs' misses, 2024-11-20 on $56.0\%$. For Claude 3.5 Sonnet, which has been retired, we substitute nothing.
Our code-free grader is BixBench's framing without the notebook, asked to map the answer; it approximates the 2026
system's grader but is not that grader. The split into moved and unchanged keys and the paper's claim family were
chosen after the v1.5 results, and the plan was not deposited in a public registry, so the test's precedence rests on
GitHub's push record, archived with the code (Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). Keys are taken as released. Dropping
the six capsules a concurrent audit examined changes no moved-key contrast of the rule or of a grader given the
notebook by more than $1.8$ points. On the $26$ numeric items whose keys BixBench-Verified-50's experts re-checked,
the seven configurations' forced-choice excess, $+13.5$ $[+6.1,+20.1]$, and the rule's gain on the $14$ moved keys
among them, $+19.6$ $[+5.0,+31.1]$, are as on all items, though gpt-5.1's excess there is $+1.9$ $[-11.3,+13.5]$
(Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). The study covers one benchmark's secondary metric, on $105$ and $159$ items. The
code, the recorded runs and grades, and every analysis are at
\url{https://github.com/Vijayavallabh/forced-choice-tolerance}; all results can be recomputed from them without a GPU.

\paragraph{What the options alone reveal.} An item presents a set $V$ of $k$ options, one of which is the key $Y$. A
grader that sees only $V$ (neither the question nor the data) can exceed chance by at most
\begin{equation}
\Gamma \;=\; \mathbb{E}_V\Big[\max_{v\in V}\Pr(Y=v\mid V)\Big]-\tfrac1k ,
\label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
the population value of the choices-only baseline \citep{choicesonly}. $\Gamma$ depends on the law that generated
the options, so it cannot be computed for a released file; the rank rules of Section~\ref{sec:theory} bound it from
below. A grader's margin over chance shows that the options reveal the key, but chance performance does not show that
they conceal it:

\begin{lemma}[when the options reveal nothing]
\label{lem:flat}
$\Gamma=0$ if and only if $\Pr(Y=v\mid V)=\tfrac1k$ for almost every presented $V$, and then every grader
that sees only the options is correct with probability exactly $\tfrac1k$, whatever statistic of the options
it uses.
\end{lemma}

\paragraph{Lemma~\ref{lem:flat}.} $\max_{v\in V}\Pr(Y=v\mid V)\ge\frac1k\sum_{v\in
V}\Pr(Y=v\mid V)=\frac1k$ pointwise, so $\Gamma\ge0$ always and $\Gamma=0$ forces
$\max_{v}\Pr(Y=v\mid V)=\frac1k$ almost surely, which for $k$ non-negative numbers summing to one
means all of them equal $\frac1k$. Conversely a flat posterior makes the maximum $\frac1k$ on every
$V$. A grader that sees only $V$ picks $\pi(V)\in V$ by any rule and any randomness, and
$\Pr[\pi(V)=Y]=\mathbb{E}_V\Pr(Y=\pi(V)\mid V)=\frac1k$; the statistic it uses never enters.

\paragraph{The rank rule's floor.} A bracketed key has one of the $k-2$ middle ranks, so if a share $\beta$ of a
file's keys are bracketed, $\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}p_j=\beta$ and the largest of those $k-2$ terms is at least their mean,
$\beta/(k-2)$. The rule that always selects that rank uses only $V$ and scores $p_j$, and no rule using
only $V$ scores more than $\mathbb{E}_V[\max_v\Pr(Y=v\mid V)]$, so $\Gamma\ge\max_jp_j-\tfrac1k\ge
\beta/(k-2)-\tfrac1k$. On v1.5's $105$ numeric items $\beta=0.810$, a floor of $0.405-0.25=+15.5$ points,
and the second rank holds $51.4\%$, $+26.4$; on v1.0's $159$, $\beta=0.610$, and the floor and the ceiling
are $+5.5$ and $+5.8$. Both are statements about these finite files; a population $\Gamma$ carries the
sampling uncertainty of $\max_jp_j$. A rank uniform over the $k-2$ middle ranks makes $p_j=1/(k-2)$ for each middle
$j$ and $0$ at the extremes, so the best rank rule scores $1/(k-2)$ and $\Gamma\ge1/(k-2)-1/k=2/(k(k-2))$; a rank
uniform over all $k$ makes every $p_j=1/k$ and leaves $2/k$ of the keys at the extremes.

\paragraph{Observation~\ref{obs:bracket}.} For $a,v$ of the same sign write $r=|a|/|v|$: $d(a,v)=|r-1|/(r+1)=
\tanh(|\log r|/2)$, increasing in $|\log|a|-\log|v||$, and $d(a,v)=1$ when the signs differ or one of them is
zero. Let the key $y\ne0$ be the largest in magnitude among the options of its sign, and $a$ an answer of that
sign with $|a|>|y|$. Every other option $v$ of that sign has $|v|<|y|<|a|$, so $\log|y|$ lies between $\log|v|$
and $\log|a|$, $|\log|a|-\log|y||<|\log|a|-\log|v||$ and $d(a,y)<d(a,v)$; every option of the other sign, or
zero, has $d(a,v)=1>d(a,y)$. The smallest in magnitude is symmetric, with $|a|<|y|<|v|$. For a positive key
largest in magnitude means largest in value; for a negative key it means smallest in value, and the key largest in
value is the smallest in magnitude among the negative options. For $a$ and $v$ of the key's sign,
$d(a,y)<d(a,v)$ exactly when $\log|a|$ lies on $y$'s side of the midpoint of $\log|y|$ and $\log|v|$, the
logarithm of the geometric mean $\sqrt{|y||v|}$. With the key's nearest options of its sign at magnitudes
$|y|/\rho_-$ and $|y|\rho_+$, a miss of its sign is therefore nearest the key exactly when
$|y|/\sqrt{\rho_-}<|a|<|y|\sqrt{\rho_+}$; options further out are further from such an answer, and options of the
other sign are at distance $1$. An extreme key has no such neighbour on its open side, and by the argument above
every answer of its sign beyond it is nearest it; the counts of extreme keys and of the rank rule's gain are those of
the previous paragraph. The argument holds for any distance that increases with separation on the line. For the rule
a key is extreme when it is the smallest or largest in magnitude among the options of its sign, which differs from
its rank among the values where zero or a negative option lies below a positive key ($2$ of v1.5's $105$ released
keys, $2$ of v1.0's $159$) and where the key is zero ($4$ and $2$), at distance $1$ from every nonzero answer. The
tests use the rank among the values, as specified. $9$ of the $105$ keys are zero or negative; an answer at distance
$1$ from every option is scored as a tie split evenly, the expected score of a random tie-break.

\paragraph{How far a grader follows the nearest option.} Graders follow the nearest option only in part.

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:lambda}
Let a forced grader, on a miss whose number has a single nearest option, select no option with probability
$\delta$ and, when it selects one, the nearest option with probability $q$ and each other option with probability
$(1-q)/(k-1)$, whatever the options. Then a change of options that makes the key the nearest option for a share
$\Delta$ of the misses
raises the share it accepts by $\lambda\Delta$, where $\lambda=(1-\delta)(qk-1)/(k-1)$.
\end{remark}

A grader of this form bears the rule's cost in proportion to its \emph{proximity weight} $\lambda$: in full if it
always selects the nearest option, not at all if it selects the nearest no more often than any other ($q=1/k$),
and less as it refuses more.

\paragraph{Remark~\ref{rem:lambda}.} On a miss whose number has a single nearest option the grader accepts with
probability $(1-\delta)q$ if that option is the key and $(1-\delta)(1-q)/(k-1)$ if it is not. A change of options
that moves a share $\Delta$ of these misses from the second case to the first raises acceptance by
$\Delta(1-\delta)\big(q-(1-q)/(k-1)\big)=\lambda\Delta$; misses whose nearest option is the key under both option
sets, or under neither, contribute nothing. We measure $q$ as the share of a grading's selections on misses that
are the nearest option to the number the tolerance reads, and $\delta$ as the share of its gradings of those misses
that select none, every option set pooled; ties in $d$, as for an answer of zero, which every positive option is at
distance $1$ from, are broken by $|a-v|$. Real graders are not of this form: they may read a number that the
tolerance does not, prefer an option for reasons other than its distance, or find the key in the notebook, so we
use $\lambda$ to order graders, and set its prediction beside each observed gain only in Table~\ref{tab:variants}.
Estimated instead from a grading's selections on the unchanged and inward keys alone, $\lambda$ moves by a median
of $0.006$ and at most $0.083$. Its predictions of the moved keys' gain err by $3.9$ $[3.3,5.8]$ points on average
over the $42$ gradings that also graded those keys, against $3.9$ in sample, $9.7$ $[7.8,12.5]$ for a constant
share of the rule's gain and $3.4$ $[2.7,9.1]$ for the mean share of the grading's kind (code-free, with the
notebook, or with the within-$5\%$ option). It under-predicts the code-free gradings by $5.3$ points and
over-predicts those with the within-$5\%$ option by $2.8$, and the observed gain lies within the interval of its
prediction for $18$ of the $42$.

\paragraph{Exchangeability is sufficient and strictly stronger.} If the $k$-tuple
$(\text{key},\text{distractor}_1,\dots,\text{distractor}_{k-1})$ is exchangeable, then the key is
equally likely to be any element of $V$, so $\Gamma=0$. The converse fails. Draw a $k$-subset $V$
from any law, let the key be uniform on $V$, and list the remaining $k-1$ values in increasing
order. The posterior is flat and $\Gamma=0$, while the permutation that swaps two distractor slots
sends the law to one supported where the order is reversed, a set of probability zero. An exact enumeration
at $k=3$ over ten values gives $\Gamma=1\times10^{-15}$ with the tuple not exchangeable. The presentation
order is therefore not what has to be randomised, and randomising it, as BixBench does, does not give
$\Gamma=0$.

\paragraph{How distinctness is enforced.} If the key and the $k-1$ distractors are drawn independently
from one law $\kappa$ and the whole $k$-tuple is conditioned on being distinct, the tuple is exchangeable,
so the key is equally likely to be each presented value and $\Gamma=0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:flat}. Drawing
the key and then redrawing each distractor \emph{while it equals the key} or an earlier distractor, a natural
way to write the loop, conditions each draw on the key and removes the key's own mass from the pool, which
tilts the posterior toward the values $\kappa$ favours (Table~\ref{tab:distinctness}). The leak grows with how
concentrated $\kappa$ is: over six values with $\kappa_j\propto e^{-sj}$, it is $+7.3$, $+17.4$ and $+28.5$ points
when the largest share is $0.28$, $0.41$ and $0.53$, while the jointly conditioned draw stays at zero. We do
not know how BixBench's distractors were drawn, and nothing here shows that its rank skew arose this way.

\paragraph{Intervals.} Every interval is a percentile cluster bootstrap over the file's own clusters
(capsules for BixBench, subjects for the MMLU files). Unless its caption calls it plain, it is read at the
nominal level whose coverage reaches $95\%$ on those clusters, and coverage is measured, not assumed, in one of
two ways. For a margin or a difference over a whole file, files are simulated from each condition's own cluster
sizes and between-cluster spread, with a beta-binomial matched by moments, and the bootstrap is run on them
exactly as in the analysis: a nominal $95\%$ interval covers about $90\%$ on MMLU-Pro, so its margins are read at
$98.5\%$ and its differences at $98.25\%$, and BixBench's at $96.75$ and $96.0\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:bootcal}). For a
paired difference per question, and for any subset of a file (the moved keys, or the numeric or non-numeric
questions), a double bootstrap measures coverage on the capsules' own values: each of at least $2{,}000$ resamples of
the capsules is bootstrapped in turn, and the level is the lowest, on a grid from $80$ to $99.995\%$, at which the
resamples' own intervals hold the full sample's estimate $95\%$ of the time. Where no level on the grid does, the
estimate is given without an interval and marked $^{\ddagger}$. Kendall's $\tau$ over seven configurations takes few
values, so its level is never set below $95\%$. When a paired difference is exactly zero on most capsules (none of
their runs is read differently), a resample of only those capsules has a mean of exactly zero; that end of the
interval is set to zero whenever the chance of such a resample exceeds the tail, where Monte Carlo draws can miss it
(Qwen3-235B-A22B's moved keys, Table~\ref{tab:replication}). A control file, with the key placed uniformly among
options drawn from the file's own pool, has $\Gamma=0$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:flat} a grader that sees only the options
has an expected accuracy of exactly $\tfrac1k$ on it; control files check the procedure and are not subtracted. Of the
$26$ control files behind Table~\ref{tab:gridbix}, $3$ fall outside the central $95\%$ of their exact
key-randomisation null, all three in cells that report a refusal rate.

\paragraph{A test that needs no calibration.} The paired contrasts of Section~\ref{sec:cost} are also
tested by flipping the signs of whole capsules. Under the null hypothesis that the contrast is $\delta$, each
capsule's summed deviation from $\delta$ is assumed symmetric about zero; the p-value is the share of sign
patterns whose sum is at least as far from zero as the observed one, over every pattern up to $20$ capsules
and $200{,}000$ random patterns beyond. The test assumes nothing about the spread of capsules, and inverting
it gives an interval, which on simulated files shaped like the moved keys ($37$ items in $21$ capsules, a
true contrast of $15$ points, the items drawn independently of their capsule) covers the truth in $197$ of $200$
files. Its p-values for $U-P$ on the moved
keys are the last column of Table~\ref{tab:readersfull}; under the nearest-option rule, on the pre-specified test's
data, it gives $p<0.001$ on the published runs and on the new seeds, and $p=0.126$ for Qwen3-235B-A22B with the data,
whose interval also reaches zero (Table~\ref{tab:replication}).

\section{BixBench's no-data conditions, and the forced-choice condition rewritten}
\label{app:arms}

\paragraph{The no-data conditions as released.} With each forced-choice run, BixBench released the same models on the
same questions without options, and
with an ``insufficient information'' option added (Table~\ref{tab:arms}). On v1.5, gpt-4o and Claude 3.5
Sonnet each state the answer unaided on $6$ of $205$ questions ($2.9\%$); on $41.0$ and $54.6\%$ they state
that the value cannot be determined without the data, and among the replies that attempt an answer they are
correct on $5.0$ and $6.5\%$. Given a refusal option, they take it on $90.2$ and $78.5\%$. Forced to choose
among four options, they are correct on $74$ and $70$ ($36.1$ and $34.1\%$), $+11.1$ $[+3.8,+18.8]$ and $+9.1$
$[+2.5,+16.4]$ points above chance; corrected across the paper's ten headline claims, gpt-4o's margin
survives and Claude's interval reaches $-0.2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). What the models can state does not
account for this margin. A model that knew the answers it states unaided and guessed among four on the rest
would score $2.2$ points above chance; one that also knew the questions it declines as often as those it
attempts, $3.7$ and $4.8$. Where the two models state a number, the option nearest it is the key on $19.3$ and
$11.0\%$ of v1.5's numeric questions, where a random option would be on $25\%$, while their forced choice on
those questions is correct on $37.1$ and $36.6\%$. These bound what the models can state, not what they know: a
model may recognise the key among four values without being able to state it. Memory of the benchmark itself is
excluded by the dates: the documented knowledge cutoffs of the two models, October 2023 for gpt-4o and April 2024
for Claude 3.5 Sonnet, precede BixBench's first release in February 2025 \citep{bixbench}. What they know of the
biology behind the questions is not excluded, and these conditions cannot separate it from a sense of which values
are plausible.

Nor is the margin shown to come from the rank cue (Table~\ref{tab:attribution}). A model that drew its whole
v1.5 margin from the rank would be correct far more often where the key is second-smallest ($45.9$ and $35.1$ points
more often, to score what these two score), and would gain nothing on questions whose options are not numbers. The two
models are $7.8$ and $13.4$ points \emph{less} accurate where the key is second-smallest, and as far above chance on
the $100$ non-numeric questions ($+10.0$ and $+9.0$) as on the numeric ones ($+12.1$ and $+9.3$). The cells are small
($13$, $54$, $31$ and $7$ items), so this bounds the rank's share rather than excluding it: at the upper ends of the
corrected intervals (Table~\ref{tab:claims}), a preference for the second-smallest option could explain at most $45\%$
of gpt-4o's numeric margin and $59\%$ of Claude's. A later gpt-4o (version 2024-11-20; Table~\ref{tab:gptnodata}), run
again without the data, is at chance with the question withheld ($+1.0$ and $+1.4$ points on the two releases) and
selects the second-smallest option on $24.1\%$ of v1.5's items, where the key lies on $51.4\%$. With the question,
under BixBench's template, redrawing every distractor changes its margin by no significant amount on either release,
so there the margin rests on the question and the key's value; under our chat prompt redrawing removes $8.9$
$[+2.5,+14.6]$ of v1.0's $+14.6$. The open-weight models agree (below and Appendix~\ref{app:reader}): none selects the
second-smallest option on more than $33.3\%$ of items without the question, and solved v1.5 items in context teach
eight nothing about the key's rank. The cue is available, worth $+26.4$ points to a rule, but no model we tested is
shown to use it.

\paragraph{Approximate knowledge.} A forced choice could exceed what a model states without the data if the
model knows roughly where a value lies. BixBench's own replies without options allow a test that involves no
model of ours: a reply that states a number, and does not say that the value cannot be determined, is mapped to
the released option nearest that number. On v1.5's $105$ numeric questions gpt-4o states a number on $33.3\%$
and Claude 3.5 Sonnet on $39.0\%$, and the option nearest it is the key on $19.3$ $[7.1,33.3]$ and $11.0\%$
$[2.9,19.4]$ of those, where a random option is correct on $25\%$. A model that picked, when forced, the option
nearest the number it states and guessed elsewhere would score $23.1$ and $19.5\%$; the forced-choice runs
score $14.0$ $[+4.2,+23.6]$ and $14.8$ $[+4.8,+25.8]$ points more. On v1.0's $159$ the two state a number on
$34.0$ and $31.4\%$, nearest the key on $20.4$ and $17.0\%$ of those; the forced-choice runs score $6.8$
$[-0.9,+14.9]$ and $5.2$ $[-1.7,+12.3]$ points above that prediction, and the forced choice, whose order v1.0
records, is the option nearest the model's own stated number on $35.2$ $[23.2,47.2]$ and $27.0\%$
$[15.6,38.5]$ of those questions. Intervals are plain $95\%$ cluster bootstraps.

\paragraph{Asked for a number.} BixBench's condition without options lets a model decline. Shown each numeric
question without the data or the options and asked for its answer as a number, gpt-4o 2024-11-20 gives one on
$104$ of v1.5's $105$ and on all of v1.0's $158$; told as well that the data are unavailable and that a best
estimate is required, on all of both. Its number is within $5\%$ of the key on $1.9\%$ of v1.5's questions under
either prompt ($8.2$ and $3.2\%$ on v1.0), and the released option nearest it is the key on $17.3$ $[9.4,26.2]$
and $13.1\%$ $[7.4,19.5]$ ($22.8$ and $18.8\%$ on v1.0), where a random option is correct on $25\%$. Forced to
choose among the same options under BixBench's template with the question, it selects the key on $30.8\%$
$[25.2,36.1]$ of v1.5's questions and $35.2\%$ of v1.0's (Table~\ref{tab:gptnodata}): $13.4$ $[+4.5,+21.9]$ and
$17.7$ $[+9.2,+25.7]$ points more on v1.5 than choosing the option nearest its own number would score, and
$12.4$ and $16.4$ more on v1.0. Asked for a number, its no-data baseline is near zero; its forced-choice margin
needs the options and, by Table~\ref{tab:gptnodata}, rests on the question and the key's value among them, not on
their rank. Plain $95\%$ cluster bootstraps.

\paragraph{BixBench's components.} The agent is BixBench's data-analysis agent at the version the benchmark
specifies (1.5.0): its system prompt and open-answer task template, formatted as BixBench's code formats
them; its three tools, for editing a notebook cell, listing the working directory and submitting an answer;
at most $40$ steps at temperature $1.0$, with earlier notebook states hidden; and the capsule laid out as the
benchmark lays it out. Every cell runs in a rebuild of BixBench's notebook image from its own recipe, with the
capsule mounted read-only. Each run is graded in the two ways BixBench publishes: open-ended, by the v1.5
graders, and through the options, by BixBench's MCQ grader, a second model shown the notebook, the question
with the options shuffled, and the agent's answer.

\paragraph{Our choices.} In the text protocol, three model families call the tools in text, because their
native formats differ; each is served in FP8 with vLLM and grades its own runs, as the published runs were
graded by the model that made them. The published protocol is BixBench's ReAct agent at the versions the
benchmark specifies; because vLLM does not support the tool-choice settings the agent requests, the
tool-selection call begins the reply with the model family's own tool-call tag. The agent's reasoning step
sends both Qwen families into a repetition loop that runs to the $4{,}096$-token reply limit (all $183$ of
Qwen2.5-72B's reasoning turns that reach the limit and $2{,}090$ of Qwen3-30B-A3B's $2{,}276$, a third of its turns);
we leave this as published. Episodes cut short ($26$ of the published protocol's $1{,}640$ by our one-hour limit on
shared GPUs, and $69$ of Qwen2.5-72B's text-protocol episodes by a GPU fault and the load it left) were run again from
the start, so no kept run was cut by a clock of ours. The \emph{no-data} condition is the same agent with an empty
working directory. Every grading call is recorded, so every number can be recomputed from the recorded runs without a
GPU.

\paragraph{Where our runs depart from the published agent.} \emph{Network}: our cells run without network access,
which the published agent had; $8$ of the published v1.0 notebooks read KEGG online or install a package from
CRAN. A failed connection or name lookup appears in $6$ to $22$ of each configuration's $105$ with-data runs on the
numeric questions, in $16$ of gpt-5.1's $210$ and in $12$ of gpt-6-luna's $105$, and none of those runs by gpt-5.1
or gpt-6-luna lands within $5\%$ of the key. \emph{Context}: the open models are served with $32{,}768$ tokens of
context (Qwen2.5-72B and Qwen3-235B-A22B) or $65{,}536$ (the others), gpt-5.1 and gpt-6-luna with $272{,}000$,
DeepSeek-V4-Pro and Kimi-K3 with $128{,}000$. When
the history outgrows the context, the older turns are cut to a few hundred characters, keeping the system prompt,
the task and the latest turns (in $62$ and $97$ of Qwen2.5-72B's $410$ episodes under the two protocols, $102$ of
Qwen3-30B-A3B's and at most $20$ of the others'); $11$ of Qwen2.5-72B's episodes end when even that does not fit. The
notebook the agent is shown is capped at $36{,}000$ characters for the open models ($300{,}000$ for gpt-5.1 and
gpt-6-luna, $200{,}000$ for DeepSeek-V4-Pro and Kimi-K3), the oldest outputs shortened first, and every output at
$6{,}000$. \emph{Kernel}: a persistent kernel runs each new cell alone, reruns the notebook only when an earlier cell
is edited, and interrupts a cell after $10$ minutes, where the published environment re-executes the notebook after
every edit; the agent is never shown a plot, which the published environment attaches as an image. \emph{Models}:
GLM-4.5-Air runs and grades with its thinking mode off, and Qwen3-30B-A3B is its Instruct-2507 release.
\emph{Grading}: an MCQ grader's reply is capped at $1{,}536$ tokens ($4{,}096$ for GLM-4.5-Air) and the notebook it
reads at $60{,}000$ characters ($150{,}000$ for gemma-3-27b); every one of Qwen3-30B-A3B's own gradings that selects
no option was cut at that cap before its answer, which is why gemma-3-27b grades its runs. \emph{Wall clocks}: an hour
per episode, except $341$ of Qwen3-30B-A3B's $410$ episodes (six hours for $300$, three for $41$), the new seeds and
Qwen3-235B-A22B's later runs (three hours), and $19$ episodes of the other sets run again with three hours.

\paragraph{How $P$ and $U$ are built.} Both rewrites keep the question and the key and replace the three
distractors by one generator, which is asked for a rank for the key: its released rank for $P$, and a rank drawn
uniformly from the four for $U$. Neither looks at the question, the runs or any grading. When the four released
values share the key's sign, the generator places the $j$-th new distractor on each side of the key $y$,
counting outward, at a magnitude of $|y|(1+sju)$ or $|y|/(1+sju)$ with the key's sign, where $u$ is drawn
uniformly from $[0.6,1.4]$ for each distractor and $s$ is the released distractors' mean relative distance from
the key, $|v-y|/|y|$, and at least $0.05$; otherwise it places them at $y\pm Sju$, with $S$ their mean absolute
distance. Each new value is written in the notation, precision, per-cent sign and digit grouping of the released
distractor it replaces. A draw is discarded, and the whole set drawn again with the spread widened by half its
initial value, if rounding makes two options equal, changes a value's sign or moves the key off the requested
rank; the generator makes up to $24$ draws, the first twelve multiplicative when the values share a sign. When
none succeeds, $U$ tries the other ranks in random order ($3$ of v1.5's $105$ items and $10$ of v1.0's $159$
took a rank other than the one drawn), and $P$ leaves the item out ($1$ of v1.0's). Each rewrite is one draw at
a fixed seed, and regenerating the four option files reproduces them exactly. Because the generator spaces the
distractors by their mean released distance, the nearest distractor tends to lie farther from the key than in
$R$ (a median $d$ of $0.185$ in $P$ and $0.188$ in $U$, against $0.142$, over v1.5's $105$), so $P-R$ is not
zero; $U-P$, which shares the redrawing, isolates the rank.

\paragraph{Digit-matched rewrites.} $P'$ and $U'$ come from the same generator with one more condition: each new
distractor keeps the number of significant digits of the released distractor it replaces (a decimal one is
written to as many decimals as those digits need), and the drawn values are assigned to the released distractors
in order of size, so that the key keeps its standing among the options' digits. A draw that breaks any condition
is discarded as above. On v1.5, $U'$ leaves out $2$ of the $105$ numeric items and $P'$ $6$, where no draw
succeeds; on v1.0, $P'$ leaves out $2$ of $159$ and $U'$ none. The rule that selects the option with the fewest
significant digits is correct on $25.3$ and $25.5\%$ of v1.5's $P'$ and $U'$, against $25.0\%$ on $R$ and $31.7$
and $31.9\%$ on $P$ and $U$ (v1.0: $30.0$ and $30.3\%$, against $30.3\%$ on $R$ and $37.8$ and $32.2\%$ on $P$
and $U$), and the nearest distractor lies at a median $d$ of $0.207$ and $0.200$. $U'$ moves $36$ of v1.5's keys
from bracketed to extreme and $12$ inward, and leaves $55$; on v1.0, $42$, $27$ and $90$. Through $P'$ and $U'$ the
rule's $U-P$ on the moved keys, the unchanged keys and all items moves by at most $2.9$ points on the published
runs, the seven configurations, the new seeds, Qwen3-235B-A22B and gpt-5.1, and by up to $4.2$ on their inward
keys; gpt-6-luna's moves by at most $0.3$, and DeepSeek-V4-Pro's, which rests on two to four answers, falls from
$+4.7$, $+3.6$ and $+3.6$ to zero.

\paragraph{Other gradings of the same runs.} The \emph{code-free} grader receives the question with its options, in
BixBench's shuffled order, and the submitted answer, framed as BixBench's MCQ prompt frames them but without the
notebook, and is asked to map the answer to the most appropriate option, where BixBench's prompt asks for the best
answer from the notebook (Figure~\ref{fig:gradingprompts}). Its reply is constrained by the server to one option's
letter, and a reply that stops before its letter selects none. With a refusal option, BixBench's ``insufficient
information'' is a fifth option. With the
\emph{within $5\%$} option, a fifth option states that none of the other options is within $5\%$ of the proposed
answer, and the grader is asked for the option within $5\%$ of the answer if there is one and for that option
otherwise. The \emph{letter-only} grader receives BixBench's MCQ prompt with the notebook, as the other
graders do, with its reply constrained to an analysis followed by one option's letter. Every grading is at
temperature $0$, with two shuffles of each option set per run.

\section{The pre-specified test and the tolerance, in full}
\label{app:replication}

\paragraph{The hypotheses.} Before the runs of the pre-specified test were analysed, we fixed the data, the
option sets, the key groups, the grading rule, the statistics and a pass criterion for each hypothesis
(Table~\ref{tab:predictions}). The plan is dated 24 September 2026, before the published runs were analysed and
before the new runs were made. It was committed to our version history and pushed to GitHub, whose push record,
archived with the code, dates the push at 16:25 UTC, $15$ minutes before that of the first results; it was not
deposited in a public registry, so a reader of this paper cannot check its precedence independently. The published
runs were
analysed that evening, the new runs made on 24 and 25 September, and the MCQ gradings of
\S\ref{sec:cost} taken on 25 and 26 September, except the two current agents' runs, made and graded on 27 September, and
DeepSeek-V4-Pro's rerun, made and graded on 1 October. Episodes of the new runs that were cut by the run's own
wall-clock limit, which the protocol does not have, were rerun from the start, as for the published protocol.
The pass criterion for the unchanged keys also accepts a mean within $5$ points of zero, which lets a change
whose interval excludes zero pass; we count H2 as failed wherever its interval excludes zero, as it does on the
published runs. The last row of Table~\ref{tab:predictions} tests the same change as an equivalence claim, with
the whole interval within $\pm5$ points: no data set satisfies it.

\paragraph{What moves the unchanged keys.} H2 counts a key as unchanged when $U$ keeps it bracketed or extreme,
but an extreme key can move to the other extreme, which moves its open side. On the published runs the $11$
keys that $U$ moves from one extreme to the other gain $+19.1$ against $R$ and $+20.5$ against $P$, $2.4$ of
the unchanged keys' $+3.7$; the $50$ that keep their rank change by $+2.0$ against $R$ and $+0.2$ against $P$,
and the $25$ moved to another middle rank by $+0.4$ and $+0.2$. Where the key keeps both its rank and its class,
$U-P$ is near zero, as H4's logic requires; H2 fails on the published runs through keys whose open side $U$
moved. For Qwen3-235B-A22B the keys that keep their rank gain $+6.7$ against $R$ and $+1.7$ against $P$: there
the redrawing itself credits near misses.

\paragraph{Corrected for guessing.} Formula scoring \citep{lordformula,frary} maps a forced score $S$ over $k$
options to $(S-1/k)/(1-1/k)$, which leaves a grader that accepts every correct answer and chooses at random on
every miss at the tolerance. Table~\ref{tab:formula} splits the corrected score minus the tolerance exactly by the
kind of answer: each kind contributes $k/(k-1)$ times what the grading accepts of it beyond what that grader would,
and an empty answer, scored wrong without grading, $-1/(k-1)$ of its share. On the published runs the misses nearest
the key and those nearest another option contribute $+2.6$ and $-6.5$ points to gpt-4o's residual and $+7.8$ and
$-9.3$ to Claude 3.5 Sonnet's, which is why the corrected scores land near the tolerance; on v1.5 the misses nearest
another option ($-3.5$ to $-6.9$) and the correct answers rejected with the empty ones ($-1.3$ to $-5.4$) outweigh
those nearest the key ($+0.5$ to $+4.1$). Scoring a grade that selects no option, and an empty answer, as an omission,
as formula scoring does for an
examinee who omits, rather than as a wrong selection moves the published gpt-4o residual from $-3.9$ to $+4.0$,
gpt-5.1's from $-10.8$ to
$+1.7$ and the two current agents' from $-6.0$ and $-7.9$ to $+5.4$ and $+5.4$. gpt-4o selects no option on $24.9$,
$49.0$, $53.8$ and $47.0\%$ of their misses, the seven configurations' graders on $12.3\%$ (their residual moves to
$-3.0$), and the others on fewer than $3\%$. For a forced grader the correction is an affine map of the score, so a
difference between option sets, such as $U-P$, is multiplied by $k/(k-1)$ and kept.

\paragraph{Four more runs of Qwen3-235B-A22B.} Its two runs in the pre-specified test fail H1, H2 and H4, and its moved

the test was analysed and outside it (rollouts $2$ to $5$, with the data, on v1.5's numeric questions, served on four
H100s as its runs in the test were). A three-hour wall clock cut $2$ of their $420$ episodes, both on one question
whose R cells kept outrunning the ten-minute cell limit; they count as unanswered. The four runs are within $5\%$ of
the key on $22.9$ to $27.6\%$ of the numeric questions. On them, graded as the test grades, the moved keys gain $+9.5$
$[+0.9,+17.4]$ against $P$, on $16$ newly accepted runs, and the unchanged keys $+4.8$ $[+0.3,+10.3]$ against $R$;
pooled with the test's two runs, $+7.9$ $[+0.7,+14.7]$ and $+6.2$ $[+1.6,+11.6]$. With six runs the moved keys' gain
against $P$ excludes zero, as a lack of power in the test would leave it, but the test's verdict stands; its unchanged
keys still gain against $R$, but the $30$ that keep their rank change by $+0.3$ against $P$: the redrawing, not the
rank, moves them.

\paragraph{Robustness checks.} A single run per question reproduces the published runs' gain ($15.1$ to
$24.6$ points in $95\%$ of $2{,}000$ draws), as does $U$ redrawn with each of $100$ seeds. Redrawn with $100$ seeds by
the same procedure, $U$ moves $33$ to $60$ keys to an
extreme, and the gain on them ranges from $+13.4$ to $+24.9$ on v1.5 with the data, $+14.8$ to $+27.9$ without

three in a pilot ($23$ of v1.5's $105$ and $24$ of v1.0's $159$, the keys it flags and their neighbours alike),
changes no moved-key contrast of Tables~\ref{tab:readers2} and~\ref{tab:readersfull} or of the pre-specified test by

\paragraph{How a number is extracted.} The nearest-option rule of the pre-specified test extracts a number only
from an answer that is a number, once commas and a trailing per cent sign are removed; an answer that is a
sentence containing a number scores zero through every option set and adds nothing to a contrast between them.
The tolerance uses the last number in an answer, and treats an answer written as a fraction against a key
written as a per cent as that fraction times $100$. Table~\ref{tab:extraction} counts the answers of each kind
and computes the rule's contrasts four ways: as in the test; from the number the tolerance uses; on the answers
that contain exactly one number; and without the keys that are zero or negative ($9$ of v1.5's $105$, $10$ of
v1.0's $159$), where an answer of the other sign is at distance $1$ from every option and splits its credit. On
the moved keys no other extraction gives a smaller contrast than the test's, nor over all items does the last
number: the test's extraction is the conservative one. On the moved keys every plain $95\%$ interval
excludes zero except Qwen3-235B-A22B's, whose lower end is at zero under three of the four extractions.

\paragraph{Dependence on agent accuracy.} The gain from a hidden rank falls on misses, so an agent that misses

$5\%$ of the key more often (Spearman $\rho=-0.56$, permutation $p=0.017$), over all items it decreases faster
($\rho=-0.89$, $p<0.001$), and per miss on a moved key it also decreases ($\rho=-0.46$, $p=0.050$): agents that
land near the key more often also miss by less. Per miss it still ranges from $+2.9$ to $+31.9$, and the
published Claude 3.5 Sonnet runs, near the key more often than gpt-4o's, lose more. The nineteen configurations
are not independent: seeds and protocols share a model. With each of the eight models counted once, its
configurations averaged, the correlations are $-0.55$ on the moved keys ($p=0.17$), $-0.90$ over all items
($p=0.005$) and $-0.43$ per miss ($p=0.30$), by exact permutation: only the decrease over all items, which
fewer misses imply, survives.

\label{app:design}

\paragraph{How the designs are built.} Every design keeps each numeric item's key and redraws its distractors with
the generator of $P$ and $U$ (Appendix~\ref{app:withdata}), asked for $k-1$ distractors and a rank for the key:
drawn uniformly from all $k$ ranks (\emph{uniform}), from the $k-2$ middle ones (\emph{middle}), or the key's
released rank ($k=4$). The distractors are spaced as those of $P$ and $U$, at the released distractors' mean relative
distance, or at a fixed fraction of the key (a fifth or a tenth), each written with the significant digits of a
released distractor, cycled. An item that no draw can place at its drawn rank takes another rank its policy allows, as
$U$ does, and one that no rank can be reached for is left out (at most $2$ items of a release). The error-modelled
designs take their distractors from the numbers other agents submitted to the same question, with or without the data,
that are more than $5\%$ from the key and of its sign. Numbers within $5\%$ of each other are merged, the most
frequent first, each written with the key's significant digits, and a side the pool cannot fill is filled by the
generator. At the released rank $85$ and $90\%$ of the distractors come from the pool on v1.0 and v1.5, and at most
$6$ items of a release are left out. A run is graded through options built without its own model's numbers; the leak
is measured on the file built from every model's. The rule reads the last number in the answer and splits ties; the
four code-free graders read each run through each design twice, gpt-4o once and on a random quarter of the published
runs and every v1.5 run, with the reply constrained to one of the letters shown. Designs are compared on the runs that
every design can grade ($4{,}877$ of the $5{,}161$ published runs, $2{,}070$ of the $2{,}205$ v1.5 runs with the
data), and each grader on the runs it read through every design.

\paragraph{What they show.} Where the key never sits at an extreme, the rule accepts $12$ to $15\%$ of the misses at
every $k$, and a rule that ignores the question gains about what Observation~\ref{obs:bracket} states or
more: on held-out capsules, from $4.8$ points less to $10.4$ more. Coarsely written keys (small counts, round
percentages) cannot take every rank at close spacing, so the most frequent middle rank holds more than its uniform
share ($35.4$ against $25\%$ of v1.0's keys at $k=6$). Drawing the rank from all $k$ adds a miss acceptance that falls
with $k$ as the share of extreme keys does, while each moved key gains about the same (Table~\ref{tab:designpairs}).
Close spacing narrows what a bracketed key accepts, for the rule and for the graders, whose acceptance of correct
answers it leaves between $92.0$ and $100\%$. Distractors taken from agents' errors reject misses about as the
generator's do and leave the cost of a uniform rank in place.
