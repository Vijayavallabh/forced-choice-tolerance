\begin{abstract}
BixBench asks AI agents to analyse biological data and answer questions about it in free text. One way it grades an
answer is through multiple choice: a second model, the grader, reads the agent's notebook (its code and output) and
answer, and selects which of four options the answer matches. The grader is either forced to select an option or
allowed to refuse, and a 2026 system still reports the forced-choice score. For questions whose answer is a number, a
simpler check exists: a tolerance accepts the agent's number if it lies within $5\%$ of the correct value (the
\emph{key}). We compare the two on the runs BixBench published and on new runs of open-weight models and three current
agents. Forced to select, BixBench's grader accepts $17$ to $25\%$ of the wrong answers, about as many as a random
choice among four would. But it favours wrong answers whose nearest option is the key, so whether a wrong answer is
graded correct depends on where the options lie: on the published runs of gpt-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, it accepts
these $3.5$ and $6.0$ times as often as those nearest another option. On the three current agents' runs, forced
choice still scores $10$ to $12$ percentage points above the tolerance. The standard correction for guessing matches
the tolerance on the published runs only because opposite errors cancel. The options also give away where the key
lies, which threatens BixBench's no-data baseline, where a model chooses among the same options without the data. In
BixBench's second release, v1.5, the key is the second-smallest of the four values on $51\%$ of numeric questions,
and a rule that never reads the question can learn this rank from the benchmark's other questions. Putting the key at
a random rank would hide it, but then some keys are the smallest or largest option, and every wrong answer of the same
sign beyond such a key is nearest to it. Graders accept these wrong answers to the extent that they select the option
nearest the answer. Numeric answers should be graded by tolerance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

BixBench \citep{bixbench} tests AI agents on biological data analysis. It groups its questions into \emph{capsules},
each a dataset with questions about it. An agent analyses the dataset in a notebook, a document of code and its
output, and submits a free-text answer; one agent's attempt at one question is a \emph{run}. BixBench grades the
answer in two ways. \emph{Open-ended} grading compares it with the correct answer. \emph{Multiple-choice} grading
turns it into a choice: a second model, the \emph{multiple-choice grader}, receives the notebook, the question with
four options and the agent's answer, and selects the option that best matches the answer. One option, the
\emph{key}, is correct; the other three are \emph{distractors}. The grader is either \emph{forced} to select one of
the four or allowed to \emph{refuse} by selecting a fifth option, ``Insufficient information to answer the
question''. The same options serve a second purpose: in BixBench's \emph{no-data baseline}, a model answers each
question by choosing among them without the data, and BixBench reads a score above chance there as recall.

A forced grader must select an option even when the answer matches none, so it can grade a wrong answer as
correct. BixBench's authors call the options only ``a useful proxy'', with scores ``marginally better than random'',
yet the forced-choice score remains in use. A 2026 system, BioAgent Deep Research \citep{bioagents}, reports $64.4\%$
forced-choice and $48.8\%$ open-ended accuracy on v1.5, BixBench's second release (v1.0 was the first). Its
grader sees the answer and options but not the notebook, yet its paper compares this score with
published ones whose grader saw the notebook. No one has measured how much of such a score comes from wrong answers
the grader accepts.

We measure it on the questions whose options are all numbers, the \emph{numeric items}: $159$ in v1.0 and $105$ in
v1.5. On these, correctness needs no grader. A \emph{tolerance} accepts the submitted number, the last number in the
answer, if it lies within $5\%$ of the key \citep{scibench}. We call any answer the tolerance rejects, including one
with no number, a \emph{wrong answer}. The agent never sees the options, so after a run we can grade the same answer
again through other options with the same key (Figure~\ref{fig:design}).

For a grader that selects the option nearest the answer, where a wrong answer falls among the options decides its
grade (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}a). Suppose the options are $1$, $2$, $4$ and $8$, and the key is $2$. Such a grader
accepts $2.5$, which is $25\%$ off, and rejects $5$, which is nearer $4$. Now suppose the key is $8$. Every answer
above $8$ is nearer $8$ than any other option, so the same grader accepts $10$ and $1{,}000$ alike. A key with
distractors on both sides is \emph{bracketed}, and a far-off answer on either side is nearer a distractor. A key that
is the smallest or largest option is \emph{extreme}, and every wrong answer beyond it, on its \emph{open side}, is
nearest the key. The key's position can also give the key away. Its \emph{rank} is its place among the option values
sorted from smallest to largest. If keys favour one rank, a model in the no-data baseline beats chance by always
selecting that rank. We find that:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\topsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
\item forced choice accepts about as many wrong answers as a random choice would, but favours those whose nearest
option is the key (\S\ref{sec:withdata}, Table~\ref{tab:released});
\item the correction for guessing matches the tolerance only because errors cancel (\S\ref{sec:guessing});
\item v1.5's options reveal the key's rank (\S\ref{sec:leak}, Figure~\ref{fig:overview}b);
\item drawing the rank at random would hide it, but leaves some keys extreme, and keeping keys off the extremes
reveals their rank again. A grader that \emph{follows the nearest option}, always selecting the option nearest the
submitted number, accepts every wrong answer beyond an extreme key, so hiding the rank conflicts with rejecting wrong
answers (Observation~\ref{obs:bracket});
\item real graders accept these far-off answers to the extent that they follow the nearest option (\S\ref{sec:cost}),
and no design of the option values escapes this (\S\ref{sec:design}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\emph{Grading free-text answers.} Comparing an answer with the correct one agrees with human grading where multiple
choice does not \citep{answermatching,openstyle}; for a number, the comparison is a tolerance \citep{scibench}.
Automatic answer checkers miss answers written in another form, or accept wrong ones
\citep{xverify,compassverifier,verifierpitfalls}. Models used as judges are biased and lenient
\citep{llmjudge,fairevaluators,judgingjudges}. The standard correction for guessing, formula scoring
\citep{lordformula,frary}, assumes that guesses fall on the options at random.

\emph{What the options reveal.} A no-data baseline asks what a model can answer from part of its input, like the
hypothesis-only baseline in language inference \citep{hypothesisonly,artifacts}; the options alone can be enough
\citep{choicesonly,shortcut,rightwrong,niven}. ``Choose the middle value'' is a cue that test-takers learn
\citep{testwiseness}. Shuffling the order in which the options are shown removes cues in an option's position
\citep{attalibarhillel,selectionbias,positionsensitivity}, but not this one, which lies in the values themselves.
Work on distractors asks how well they attract test-takers who do not know the answer
\citep{haladynataxonomy,haladynarodriguez,distractorgeneration,distractorfunction,howmanyoptions,threeoptions}, and
contamination tests ask whether a model has seen the test
\citep{memorisationexploitation,contaminationsurvey,taskcontamination,minkprob,memorisation,timetravel,tsguessing,exchangeablecontamination}.
Neither asks what the options reveal without the question.

\emph{Agent benchmarks.} Agent benchmarks have been criticised for lacking held-out test sets and for grading that
misestimates performance \citep{agentsmatter,abc}. A concurrent audit of BixBench \citep{bixbenchaudit} re-derives the
answer keys of three capsules ($14$ questions) from the raw data and grades $140$ replicated agent runs on them,
taking the options as given. BixBench-Verified-50 has domain experts re-check fifty of its questions; it keeps their
distractors and grades the free-text answer, $13$ of the $50$ by a numeric range \citep{verified50}. Frontier agents
with added skills now answer $84$ to $98\%$ of them \citep{zhang2026}. Other benchmarks for scientific data analysis
grade without options \citep{corebench,discoverybench,scienceagentbench} or show the options to the model that
answers \citep{labbench}. None measures what a forced-choice grade of a free-text answer is made of.

\subsection{Why a 5\% tolerance}
\label{sec:reference}

A $5\%$ width, which the SciBench benchmark also uses, agrees with BixBench's own judgment. BixBench's authors wrote
$60$ of the v1.5 keys as ranges, and these have a median half-width of $6.1\%$. On $5{,}161$ published answers of
gpt-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, BixBench v1.0's open-ended grader and a $5\%$ tolerance disagree on only $120$
($2.3\%$). Their chance-corrected agreement, $\kappa=0.90$, is the highest of the tolerances we tried
(Table~\ref{tab:tolerance}).

\subsection{Option sets and what they reveal}
\label{sec:theory}

\paragraph{The key's rank.} Let $p_j$ be the share of items whose key has rank $j$ among the $k$ option values. A
\emph{rank rule} always selects the option at one rank $j$, ignoring the question, and so is correct on a share $p_j$
of the items. If each rank is equally likely to hold the key (a \emph{uniform} rank), every $p_j$ is $1/k$ and no
rank rule beats chance. A key is \emph{bracketed} if distractors lie on both sides of it and \emph{extreme} otherwise.

\paragraph{The nearest-option rule.} To see what the option values alone do to a grade, we use a grader with no
language model. The \emph{nearest-option rule} (\emph{the rule}) takes the submitted number $a$ and selects the option
$v$ that minimises $d(a,v)=|a-v|/(|a|+|v|)$. For values of the same sign, this is the option nearest $a$ on a log
scale, so an answer twice too large is as far off as one half too small.
In the pre-specified test (\S\ref{sec:stats}), the rule reads a number only when the whole answer is a single number
(Table~\ref{tab:extraction}), a stricter reading than the tolerance's.

\paragraph{Three option sets.} We grade every answer through three option sets with the same key. $R$ is the
\emph{released} options. $P$ and $U$ redraw every distractor; $P$ \emph{preserves} the key's rank, and $U$ draws it
\emph{uniformly}. One generator makes $P$ and $U$. It sees neither the question
nor the runs, and its nearest distractor lies farther from the key than the released one
(Appendix~\ref{app:options}), so $P$ differs from $R$ too. $U$ moves $37$ of v1.5's $105$ numeric keys from bracketed
to extreme (the \emph{moved keys}) and $12$ from extreme to bracketed (the \emph{inward keys}). It leaves $56$
bracketed or extreme (the \emph{unchanged keys}), though it may change their rank, even from one extreme to the other.

\paragraph{The cost of hiding the rank.} $P$ and $U$ differ only in how the key's rank is set. So $U-P$, the extra
share of answers a grading accepts through $U$, in percentage points (\emph{points}), is the \emph{cost} of hiding the
rank for that grading. On the moved keys it isolates the rank's effect. Over all items, it is what a
benchmark would see if it drew the rank uniformly. It is smaller there, because $U$ also moves keys inward, where the
rule and most gradings accept fewer answers.

\begin{observation}[the nearest-option rule]
\label{obs:bracket}
Let a key $y\neq0$ have its nearest options of the same sign at magnitudes $|y|/\rho_-$ and $|y|\rho_+$
($\rho_\pm>1$). The rule accepts exactly the wrong answers of $y$'s sign whose magnitude lies between
$|y|/\sqrt{\rho_-}$ and $|y|\sqrt{\rho_+}$, and, if $y$ has no such option on one side (an extreme key), also every
wrong answer of its sign beyond it on that side, however large. Among $k$ options, a key rank drawn uniformly leaves
$2/k$ of the keys extreme in expectation, and a rank kept off the extremes lets a rank rule gain at least
$1/(k-2)-1/k=2/(k(k-2))$ over chance.
\end{observation}

In words, the rule accepts a wrong answer that lies closer to the key than halfway, on a log scale, to the next option
on each side. So it rejects every far-off answer around a bracketed key, but accepts every wrong answer beyond an
extreme key, however far off. A uniform rank leaves some keys extreme. Keeping every key at one of the $k-2$ middle
ranks instead means that the most common middle rank holds at least $1/(k-2)$ of the keys, so a rank rule beats
chance. The rank cannot be both hidden and kept off the extremes (proof in Appendix~\ref{app:theory}).

\paragraph{Graders between the rule and the tolerance.} Observation~\ref{obs:bracket} constrains only a grader that
follows the nearest option. A tolerance of width $t$ rejects every answer more than $t$ from the key, whatever the
options, and a grader offered an option saying that no value is within $t$ of the answer can reject them too. We measure
how closely a model grader follows the nearest option by its \emph{proximity weight} $\lambda=(1-\delta)(qk-1)/(k-1)$. Here $q$ is the share of its selections on wrong answers that are the option
nearest the answer, and $\delta$ is the share of wrong answers on which it selects no option: even a forced grader's
reply sometimes names no option's letter. The factor $(qk-1)/(k-1)$ rescales $q$ so that chance ($q=1/k$) gives $0$
and always selecting the nearest option gives $1$; and $1-\delta$ discounts replies that select nothing. A
grader whose $q$ and $\delta$ do not depend on the options bears the rule's cost in proportion to $\lambda$: its
$U-P$ is $\lambda$ times the rule's on the same answers (Remark~\ref{rem:lambda}).

\subsection{Statistics and the pre-specified test}
\label{sec:stats}

Questions about the same dataset are not independent, so every interval in the main text comes from resampling whole
capsules. With few capsules of unequal size, an interval computed at the nominal $95\%$ level contains the true value
less than $95\%$ of the time. So we compute each interval at the higher nominal level that does contain the true
value $95\%$ of the time on data like its own, and give no interval where no level does (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).
To test a difference between two gradings of the same answers, we flip the sign of each capsule's difference at random
and see how often the mean difference is as large (the \emph{sign-flip test}). We also correct the paper's ten
headline findings, listed in Table~\ref{tab:claims}, jointly for multiple comparisons; the intervals elsewhere are not
corrected.

\paragraph{The pre-specified test.} Before analysing runs we had not yet analysed (the published runs, and new v1.5
runs made afterwards), we fixed six hypotheses about the nearest-option rule in a dated plan, each with a pass
criterion (Appendix~\ref{app:replication}, Table~\ref{tab:predictions}). Most compare $U$ with $R$, the options a
benchmark actually releases. Under the rule, $U$ should accept more answers than $R$ on the moved keys (H1), but not
on the unchanged keys (H2). Most answers it newly accepts should lie more than $25\%$ from the key and on its open side
(H3). $U$ should accept more than $P$ on the moved keys, so that the rise comes from the rank and not from the
redrawing (H4). $U$ should not accept more than $R$ on the inward keys (H5). And H1 should hold on the runs without
the data too (H6), as the rise lies in the options, not in the analysis. We chose these hypotheses, and the split into
moved and unchanged keys, after the first v1.5 results. Gradings by a model are outside the test.

We call a grading's score minus the share of answers within $5\%$ of the key its \emph{excess}: how many answers, in
points, it grades correct beyond those the tolerance accepts. Forced to choose, BixBench's grader accepts about as
many wrong answers as a random choice among four would, but which ones it accepts is not random. On the published
runs, it accepts wrong answers whose nearest option is the key $3.5$ and $6.0$ times as often as those nearest another
option (Table~\ref{tab:released}). Wrong answers nearest another option are far more common, so most of the excess
comes from them, and from answers with no number or no single nearest option. More than a quarter of gpt-4o's
published answers to numeric questions contain no number (Table~\ref{tab:extraction}). The grader accepts $29$ and
$34\%$ of the answers with no number, though the key's value is in the notebook for only $16$ and $7\%$ of them.

The excess does not come from models grading their own runs (Table~\ref{tab:runs}):
gemma-3-27b, grading all seven v1.5 configurations, finds as much excess as their own models. Graders that see
different inputs do differ. Graded by gpt-4o without the notebook, like the 2026 system's grader, the same runs score
$0.6$ to $6.5$ points more over all of BixBench's questions than graded under its prompt with the notebook
(Table~\ref{tab:allq}). The excess persists on the current agents' runs, although under the joint correction
(\S\ref{sec:stats}) gpt-6-luna's interval extends to $-1.1$. Nor does the share of wrong answers accepted fall as
agents improve: over the $22$ run sets with the data, it shows no rank correlation with the share within $5\%$
($+0.07$, $p=0.75$; Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). The excess also holds against the other references we tried
(Appendix~\ref{app:excess}). With the refusal option, the score comes close to the tolerance's, but only because the wrong
answers it accepts offset the correct answers it rejects. It rejects $8.8$ and $10.2\%$ of the correct answers on the
published runs, where it refuses on $82$ and $69\%$ of runs.

\subsection{The correction for guessing matches the tolerance only because errors cancel}
\label{sec:guessing}

Formula scoring \citep{lordformula,frary}, the standard correction for guessing, maps a forced score $S$ over $k$
options to $(S-1/k)/(1-1/k)$. A grader that accepts every correct answer and chooses at random on every wrong one
then gets, in expectation, exactly the tolerance's score. On the published runs the corrected score comes close to
the tolerance's (Table~\ref{tab:released}, \emph{corrected}), but only as a sum of opposite parts
(Table~\ref{tab:formula}). The grader accepts wrong answers nearest the key more often than one in four, and those
nearest another option less often. Compared with a random choice, the first add $2.6$ and $7.8$ points and the second
subtract $6.5$ and $9.3$; the remaining parts are smaller. On v1.5 the corrected score falls below the tolerance's,
largely because forced graders sometimes select no option. The correction counts this as a wrong guess, not a
skipped question (Appendix~\ref{app:guessing}). It never reverses the order of two scores, so it leaves every ranking
as forced choice made it.

\label{sec:leak}

v1.5's options give away the key's rank. On its $105$ four-option numeric items ($46$ capsules), the key's rank
distribution, from smallest to largest, is $(p_1,\dots,p_4)=(12.4,51.4,29.5,6.7)\%$. This is far from uniform
($p=4\times10^{-8}$, treating each capsule's items as a cluster; Figure~\ref{fig:overview}b). A rule that always
selects the second-smallest option, without reading the question, scores $+26.4$ $[+14.0,+38.6]$ points above chance.
The rank can also be learned from other questions: holding out each of the $46$ capsules in turn, the rank chosen on
the other $45$ is always the second-smallest. In v1.0, the rank does not carry over to new questions. On its $159$
numeric items, the best rank rule scores $+5.8$ on the items it was chosen on, but $-2.4$ when its rank is chosen on
the other capsules.

The skew arose in v1.5 (Table~\ref{tab:origin}). In the $24$ of v1.0's items whose distractors v1.5 rewrote, the key
is second-smallest on $19$ ($79\%$, up from $29\%$ in v1.0). Other benchmarks leak too. We surveyed nine released
multiple-choice datasets with at least $20$ items whose options are all distinct numbers
\citep{labbench,aquarat,medmcqa,medqa,mmlu,mmlupro,sciq}, BixBench's two included. In five, the key's rank departs
from uniform beyond chance when the items from one source (for BixBench, a capsule) are treated as a cluster. In four
of these (v1.5, SciQ, MMLU-Pro and MMLU), a rank chosen on some clusters stays above chance on the held-out ones
(Table~\ref{tab:survey}). No model we tested is shown to use the rank (Appendix~\ref{app:arms}). The leak threatens
BixBench's no-data baseline: a model that scores above chance there may be reading the rank rather than recalling the
answer. It does not threaten the scores with the data, since the agent never sees the options. But the same options
grade those answers, so drawing the rank at random to close the leak changes the grades too; we now ask what that
costs.

\subsection{Hiding the rank costs a grader as far as it follows the nearest option}
\label{sec:cost}

Under the nearest-option rule, hiding the rank raises the share of the seven configurations' answers graded correct by
$+16.7$ $[+10.2,+23.0]$ points on the moved keys, and by $+5.3$ $[+1.9,+8.6]$ over all numeric items
(Table~\ref{tab:cost}). As Observation~\ref{obs:bracket} predicts, of the $47$ answers that the rule accepts
through $U$ but not through $R$ across the seven configurations, $46$ lie on the key's open side and $16$ are off by
more than $100\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}). The cost falls on wrong
answers, so it shrinks as agents improve. Over the nineteen run sets with the data (without the current agents),
the rule's cost over all items falls as agents land within $5\%$ of the key more often (rank correlation $-0.89$;
Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}).

Model graders bear a share of the rule's cost, and the share rises with their proximity weight
(Figure~\ref{fig:cost}a). Answer-only graders follow the nearest option most closely and bear most of the cost.
Graders given BixBench's prompt and the notebook bear less; on the new seeds, none of their costs is significant by
the sign-flip test ($p=0.059$ to $0.861$). BixBench released its own grades only through $R$, so the cost its graders
bear on the published runs' moved keys can only be predicted from their proximity weights. The predictions, $\lambda$
times the rule's cost on each model's own runs, are $+6.9$ points for gpt-4o's grades ($\lambda=0.24$) and $+20.1$ for
Claude 3.5 Sonnet's ($\lambda=0.58$, within the answer-only graders' $0.48$ to $0.69$ on those runs), though such
predictions err by $3.9$ points on average (Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). With the within-$5\%$ option, answer-only graders
bear none of the cost: hiding the rank changes what they accept on the moved keys by $-2.0$ to $-0.1$ points.

\paragraph{The pre-specified test.} H1, H4 and H6 pass if their interval lies above zero, H5 if its mean is at most zero,
and H2 if its interval contains zero or its mean is within $5$ points of zero (Table~\ref{tab:predictions}). Observation~\ref{obs:bracket} already fixes the direction of
H1, H3 and H5, so for these the test asks only whether the effect is large enough to detect. All three pass on the
published runs and on the new seeds (Figure~\ref{fig:test}). Of the two hypotheses whose outcome the observation does
not fix, H4 passes there too. H2 fails on the published runs: it passes only through the $5$-point clause, while its
interval excludes zero. There, $2.4$ of the unchanged keys' $+3.7$ points come from $11$ keys that $U$ moves from one
extreme to the other, which changes their open side. H6 passes on both v1.5 run sets. Qwen3-235B-A22B fails H1, H2 and
H4, because the generator's farther distractors already make the rule accept many of its wrong answers that are close
to the key, whatever the key's rank (Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). If the rule reads the last
number instead, as the tolerance does, no verdict changes (Table~\ref{tab:lastnumber}).

\subsection{No design of the option values escapes the conflict}
\label{sec:design}

To see whether better distractors avoid the conflict, we rebuilt every numeric item's distractors under
\emph{designs} an author could choose, and regraded the same runs with the data by the rule and the four answer-only
graders (Table~\ref{tab:design}, Appendix~\ref{app:design}, Figure~\ref{fig:cost}b). A design
sets the number of options $k$ ($4$ to $10$), the key's rank (as released at $k=4$, drawn from all $k$ ranks, or
drawn from the $k-2$ ranks off the extremes) and the distractors (spaced as in $P$ and $U$, a fixed fifth or tenth of
the key's value apart, or other agents' wrong numbers for the same question).

Distractors taken from other agents' wrong numbers leave the cost in place. Drawing
their key's rank uniformly rather than at its released rank raises the rule's acceptance on the keys this moves to an
extreme by $+24.1$ $[+16.3,+33.8]$ points on the published runs and $+18.8$ $[+11.3,+28.1]$ on v1.5. More options
shrink both sides of the conflict. Against a rank kept off the extremes, a uniform rank costs the rule $11.1$ and $8.4$
points over all items at $k=4$ (v1.0 and v1.5), and $6.1$ and $4.5$ at $k=8$. These exceed Table~\ref{tab:cost}'s costs because every key kept off the extremes is bracketed. A rank kept off the extremes
reveals at least $25$ points at $k=4$, and $4.2$ at $k=8$ (Tables~\ref{tab:design} and~\ref{tab:designpairs}). No
design removes both the leak and the cost for a grader that follows the nearest option. With the within-$5\%$ option,
the rank can be drawn uniformly without the cost, but such graders reject $6.6$ to $17.9\%$ of correct answers
(Appendix~\ref{app:replication}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Recommendations.} They apply beyond BixBench: other released datasets reveal the key's rank too
(\S\ref{sec:leak}), and Observation~\ref{obs:bracket} holds wherever free-text numeric answers are mapped to options by
nearness. \emph{Grade numeric answers by tolerance}, reading the number the same way for every grading
(Table~\ref{tab:extraction}). \emph{Report how many answers contain no number}: a tolerance rejects them, but forced
choice can accept them (\S\ref{sec:withdata}). \emph{Do not correct for guessing} (\S\ref{sec:guessing}), \emph{and do
not compare forced-choice scores from graders that see different inputs} (\S\ref{sec:withdata}). \emph{If free-text
answers must be graded through options, add an option saying that no value is within the tolerance}, and report how
many correct answers the grader then rejects (\S\ref{sec:design}). \emph{For a no-data baseline}, where the model
chooses among the options itself, \emph{draw the key's rank uniformly} and publish how often the key takes each rank
(\S\ref{sec:leak}).

\paragraph{Limitations.} No human audited the accepted wrong answers or the tolerance's verdicts. Our open-weight
agents, run at temperature $1.0$ (more varied output), are weak: with the data, their
number is within $5\%$ of the key on only $2$ to $24\%$ of numeric items (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}). Only the three
current agents do better. We substitute gpt-4o 2024-11-20 for the original 2024-08-06, and no model for the retired Claude
3.5 Sonnet. On the same wrong answers, the newer gpt-4o selects
no option more than twice as often (Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). We chose the ten findings corrected jointly after
the v1.5 results. The plan of the pre-specified test was not registered publicly, so the evidence that it preceded the
analysis rests on GitHub's record of the push, archived with the code (Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). We take the
keys as released. On the $26$ v1.5 numeric items among the questions BixBench-Verified-50's experts re-checked, the
excess and the rule's cost are about the same as on all items (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). We do not show that forced
choice reverses any published ranking of agents. The study covers one benchmark's secondary metric, its
multiple-choice score, on v1.0's $159$ and v1.5's $105$ numeric items.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Forced choice accepts wrong answers according to where they fall among the options, and no correction for guessing
removes this. Options that hide the key's rank leave some keys extreme, and a grader that follows the nearest option
then accepts every wrong answer beyond such a key on its open side, however far off. A tolerance accepts none of these
and reveals no rank. The code, runs, grades and analyses at
\url{https://github.com/Vijayavallabh/forced-choice-tolerance} recompute every result without a GPU.

\subsection{Runs and gradings}
\label{app:withdata}

\emph{The agent.} We run BixBench's data-analysis agent at version 1.5.0 of its software, as the benchmark specifies.
We keep its system prompt, its prompt template for a free-text answer and its three tools: edit a notebook cell, list
the working directory, and submit an answer. The agent takes at most $40$ steps at temperature $1.0$. Each capsule's
files are read-only, inside our rebuild of BixBench's software container. Table~\ref{tab:settings} lists every place
where our runs depart from BixBench's agent.

\emph{The models.} We serve the open-weight models with the vLLM inference server, with 8-bit floating-point (FP8)
weights. In the in-text runs, the agent has BixBench's prompts and tools, and our code reads each tool call from the
model's plain text. The ReAct runs use BixBench's own ReAct agent, which passes each tool call through the model
server's tool-calling interface, at the versions the benchmark specifies. In the seven configurations, each model
also grades its own runs, as each published run was graded by the model that made it; gemma-3-27b grades the new
seeds and Qwen3-235B-A22B's runs (Table~\ref{tab:runs}). In our \emph{no-data runs}, the same agent runs with an
empty working directory and still writes a free-text answer, which we grade like the others.

\emph{The gradings.} Each run is graded in the two ways BixBench publishes: open-ended, by BixBench v1.5's graders,
and through multiple choice, by its multiple-choice grader (prompts in Figure~\ref{fig:gradingprompts}). The model
judge of the open-ended graders is the configuration's own model (gpt-4o for the current agents).
Table~\ref{tab:glossary} defines the other graders. Each model grader runs at temperature $0$ and grades each run
twice per option set, with the options in two random orders.

\emph{Four ways of answering without the data.} The paper uses four, under different names. BixBench's
\emph{no-data baseline} is BixBench's own released runs, in which gpt-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet choose among the
options without the data (Appendix~\ref{app:arms}). Our \emph{no-data runs} are agents with an empty working
directory that write a free-text answer, graded like the runs with the data (H6; Table~\ref{tab:withdata}). We also
asked gpt-4o and open-weight models to choose among the options without the data, with or without the question
(Table~\ref{tab:gptnodata}, Figure~\ref{fig:norank}). Last, we asked open-weight models for a number without any
options (Appendix~\ref{app:arms}).

\emph{The width.} The keys BixBench v1.5 writes as ranges have half-widths with lower and upper quartiles of $1.3$ and

\subsection{Option sets}
\label{app:options}

The rewrites $P$ and $U$ keep each item's question and key and replace its three distractors. They differ only in
the key's rank: $P$ keeps the released rank, and $U$ draws a rank uniformly from the four. One generator makes both,
and it never sees the question, the runs or any grading.

\emph{How the generator places distractors.} It spaces the new distractors outward from the key $y$, at gaps scaled
to the released ones. When the four released values share the key's sign, the $j$-th new distractor on each side of
the key, counting outward, lies at $y(1+sju)$ or $y/(1+sju)$, where $u$ is drawn uniformly from $[0.6,1.4]$. The step
$s$ is the released distractors' mean relative distance from the key, with a floor of $0.05$. Otherwise, the
$j$-th new distractor lies at $y\pm Dju$, with $D$ the released distractors' mean absolute distance from the key. Each new value is written in the notation of the distractor it
replaces. A draw that makes two options equal, changes a value's sign or moves the key off its rank is redrawn with a
wider spread, up to $24$ times. If every redraw fails, $U$ takes another rank ($3$ of v1.5's $105$ items and $10$ of
v1.0's $159$) and $P$ leaves the item out ($1$ of v1.0's). Each rewrite is one draw at a fixed seed.

\emph{What $U-P$ measures.} The generator's first step is set by the released distractors' mean distance from the
key, not their smallest, so its nearest distractor lies farther from the key than the released options' does (a median $d$ of $0.185$ in $P$ and $0.188$ in $U$, against $0.142$ in $R$, with $d$ as in
\S\ref{sec:theory}). So $P-R$ is not zero: redrawing alone can change what a grader accepts. $P$ and $U$ are separate draws from the same generator, so their distractors are
spaced alike, and $U-P$ isolates the rank up to the variation between draws (Table~\ref{tab:robust} redraws $U$ with
$100$ seeds).

\emph{Digit-matched rewrites.} In $P$ and $U$, the key tends to have fewer significant digits than the new distractors,
so it can be told apart by them. The digit-matched rewrites $P'$ and $U'$ remove that cue: each new value also takes the significant digits
of the distractor it replaces, assigned in order of size. A rule that selects the option with the fewest significant
digits is then correct on $25.3$ and $25.5\%$ of v1.5's $P'$ and $U'$, against $25.0\%$ on $R$ and $31.7$ and $31.9\%$
on $P$ and $U$. Matching the digits changes the rule's cost little. For every set of runs but DeepSeek-V4-Pro's, the
nearest-option rule's $U'-P'$ lies within $2.9$ points of its $U-P$ on the moved keys, the unchanged keys and all
items; DeepSeek-V4-Pro's cost rests on two to four answers and falls to zero.

\subsection{Statistics}
\label{app:stats}

\emph{Intervals.} Questions about the same capsule are not independent, so every interval resamples whole capsules.
Each is a percentile bootstrap over capsules: we resample the capsules with replacement, recompute the estimate each
time, and read the interval from the percentiles of the resampled estimates. Each is read at the nominal level that
covers the true value $95\%$ of the time (\S\ref{sec:stats}), not at a nominal $95\%$. The exception is a
\emph{plain} $95\%$ interval, the uncorrected range from the $2.5$th to the $97.5$th percentile.

\emph{How the level is set.} For a margin or a difference over all of a release's items, the level comes from
simulated data. The simulation keeps each data set's own capsule sizes and its spread of accuracy between capsules
(a beta-binomial distribution matched to the observed mean and variance). For example, v1.5's $46$ capsules, of
unequal size, carry as much information as $29.6$ equal ones (Kish's effective sample size), and its margins and differences are read at
$96.75$ and $96.0\%$. For a paired difference per question, and for any subset of a release's items, a nested
resampling (a double bootstrap) sets the level. It is the lowest, on a grid from $80$ to $99.995\%$, at which the
intervals of at least $2{,}000$ resamples of the capsules hold the full sample's estimate $95\%$ of the time. Where no
level on the grid does, we give the estimate without an interval and mark it $^{\ddagger}$.

\emph{Intervals that end at zero.} When a paired difference is exactly zero on most capsules, a resample can draw
only those capsules and give exactly zero. If more resamples than the interval's tail share give exactly zero, that
percentile is itself zero, which is why several intervals end at $+0.0$.

\emph{The sign-flip test.} The test (\S\ref{sec:stats}) asks whether a paired difference could arise by chance. It
compares the difference's mean with the means obtained after flipping the sign of each capsule's difference, over
every pattern up to $20$ capsules and $200{,}000$ random patterns beyond. It assumes only that each capsule's
difference is symmetric about the true value. Turned into an interval, it contained the true value in $197$ of $200$
simulated data sets built like the moved keys.

\emph{The joint correction.} We correct ten headline findings jointly (Table~\ref{tab:claims}) for multiple
comparisons: each is read at the level that attains $1-0.05/10=99.5\%$ coverage on its own data (a Bonferroni
correction). So the findings that survive hold together, and a finding with several parts survives only if every
part does. We chose the ten after seeing the results, so the correction guards against chance among them, but not
against how they were chosen. The pre-specified test is not among them.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:theory}

This appendix proves the paper's formal statements. It first bounds how much the options alone can reveal about the
key, then proves Observation~\ref{obs:bracket} and Remark~\ref{rem:lambda}, and ends with why shuffling the displayed
order of the options does not hide the key's rank.

\paragraph{What the options alone reveal.} We first bound how far above chance a grader can score when it sees only an
item's options, and neither the question nor the data. An item presents a set $V$ of $k$ options, one of which is the
key $Y$. A grader that sees only $V$ can exceed chance by at most
\begin{equation}
\Gamma \;=\; \mathbb{E}_V\Big[\max_{v\in V}\Pr(Y=v\mid V)\Big]-\tfrac1k ,
\label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
the best margin that a choices-only baseline \citep{choicesonly}, which answers from the options alone, could reach
on unlimited items generated the same way. $\Gamma$ depends on the process that generated the options, so it cannot be
computed for a released dataset. The rank rules of \S\ref{sec:theory} bound it from below. A margin over chance
shows that the options reveal the key. A score at chance does not show that they conceal it, since another grader
might do better. The options conceal the key only in the following case:

\begin{lemma}[when the options reveal nothing]
\label{lem:flat}
$\Gamma=0$ if and only if $\Pr(Y=v\mid V)=\tfrac1k$ for almost every presented $V$, and then every grader
that sees only the options is correct with probability exactly $\tfrac1k$, whatever statistic of the options
it uses.
\end{lemma}

\paragraph{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:flat}.} For every $V$, the largest of the $k$ probabilities is at least their
mean: $\max_{v\in V}\Pr(Y=v\mid V)\ge\frac1k\sum_{v\in V}\Pr(Y=v\mid V)=\frac1k$. So $\Gamma\ge0$ always, and
$\Gamma=0$ forces $\max_{v}\Pr(Y=v\mid V)=\frac1k$ with probability one. For $k$ non-negative numbers summing to one,
this means all of them equal $\frac1k$. Conversely, if every option is equally likely to be the key given $V$, the
maximum is $\frac1k$ on every $V$. A grader that sees only $V$ picks some $\pi(V)\in V$, by any rule and with any
randomness. Then $\Pr[\pi(V)=Y]=\mathbb{E}_V\Pr(Y=\pi(V)\mid V)=\frac1k$, so the statistic it uses never enters.

\paragraph{The rank rule's floor.} Keeping every key off the extremes guarantees that a rank rule beats chance. This
paragraph proves the last part of Observation~\ref{obs:bracket} and bounds $\Gamma$ from below by the shares of keys
at each rank. On a released dataset the observed shares include chance variation, so the bound computed from them is
an in-sample gain; Table~\ref{tab:survey} sets it beside the gain that chance alone gives. A bracketed key has one of the $k-2$ middle ranks. So if a share $\beta$ of a dataset's keys are bracketed,
then $\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}p_j=\beta$, and the largest of those $k-2$ terms is at least their mean, $\beta/(k-2)$. The rule
that always selects that rank uses only $V$ and scores $p_j$. No rule using only $V$ scores more than
$\mathbb{E}_V[\max_v\Pr(Y=v\mid V)]$. Therefore $\Gamma\ge\max_jp_j-\tfrac1k\ge\beta/(k-2)-\tfrac1k$
(Table~\ref{tab:survey} gives both bounds for nine datasets). A rank uniform over the $k-2$ middle ranks makes
$p_j=1/(k-2)$ for each middle $j$, so $\Gamma\ge1/(k-2)-1/k=2/(k(k-2))$. A rank uniform over all $k$ ranks instead
makes every $p_j=1/k$ and leaves $2/k$ of the keys at the extremes.

\paragraph{Proof of Observation~\ref{obs:bracket}.} The proof rests on one fact: for numbers of the same sign, $d$
measures distance on a log scale. So a wrong answer of the key's sign is nearest the key exactly when it lies closer to
the key, on a log scale, than halfway to the next option on that side.

\emph{$d$ is a log-scale distance.} For $a,v$ of the same sign, write $r=|a|/|v|$. Then
$d(a,v)=|r-1|/(r+1)=\tanh(|\log r|/2)$, which increases with $|\log|a|-\log|v||$. When the signs differ, or one of
the two numbers is zero, $d(a,v)=1$.

\emph{Extreme keys.} Let the key $y\ne0$ be the largest in magnitude among the options of its sign, and let $a$ be an
answer of that sign with $|a|>|y|$. Every other option $v$ of that sign has $|v|<|y|<|a|$. So $\log|y|$ lies between
$\log|v|$ and $\log|a|$, which gives $|\log|a|-\log|y||<|\log|a|-\log|v||$ and $d(a,y)<d(a,v)$. Every option of the
other sign, or zero, has $d(a,v)=1>d(a,y)$. So $a$ is nearest the key. The case of the smallest magnitude is
symmetric, with $|a|<|y|<|v|$. For a positive key, largest in magnitude means largest in value; for a negative key,
it means smallest in value.

\emph{Bracketed keys.} For $a$ and $v$ of the key's sign, $d(a,y)<d(a,v)$ exactly when $\log|a|$ lies on $y$'s side
of the midpoint of $\log|y|$ and $\log|v|$. That midpoint is the logarithm of the geometric mean $\sqrt{|y||v|}$. Let
the key's nearest options of its sign lie at magnitudes $|y|/\rho_-$ and $|y|\rho_+$. A wrong answer of the key's
sign is therefore nearest the key exactly when $|y|/\sqrt{\rho_-}<|a|<|y|\sqrt{\rho_+}$. An extreme key has no such
neighbour on its open side, so every answer of its sign beyond it is nearest it.

\emph{Scope.} The part on extreme keys holds for any distance that grows with the gap between two numbers. The bounds
in $\sqrt{\rho_\pm}$ are specific to $d$.

\emph{Zero and negative values.} Under $d$, a key is extreme when it is the smallest or largest in magnitude among the
options of its sign. This differs from its rank among all the values when zero or a negative option lies below a
positive key ($2$ of v1.5's $105$ released keys, $2$ of v1.0's $159$) and when the key is zero ($4$ and $2$), since
zero is at distance $1$ from every nonzero answer. The pre-specified test uses the key's rank among all the values,
as its plan specified. Its rule scores an answer at distance $1$ from every option (zero, or of the other sign from every
option) as a tie among all the options, worth the expected score of a random tie-break, and rejects an answer from
which it reads no number.

\paragraph{Why the proximity weight predicts a grader's cost.} The next remark shows why a grader's proximity weight
$\lambda$ (\S\ref{sec:theory}) predicts what share of the nearest-option rule's cost it bears.

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:lambda}
Consider a forced grader with fixed probabilities $\delta$ and $q$, the same whatever the options. On a wrong answer
whose number has a single nearest option, it selects no option with probability $\delta$. When it selects one, it selects the nearest option
with probability $q$ and each other option with probability $(1-q)/(k-1)$. Suppose a change of options makes the key
the nearest option for a share $\Delta$ of these wrong answers. Then the share of them that the grader accepts rises
by $\lambda\Delta$, where $\lambda=(1-\delta)(qk-1)/(k-1)$.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Derivation of Remark~\ref{rem:lambda}.} On a wrong answer whose number has a single nearest option, the
grader accepts with probability $(1-\delta)q$ if that option is the key, and with probability $(1-\delta)(1-q)/(k-1)$
if it is not. A change of options that moves a share $\Delta$ of these wrong answers from the second case to the
first raises acceptance by $\Delta(1-\delta)\big(q-(1-q)/(k-1)\big)=\lambda\Delta$. Wrong answers whose nearest option
is the key under both option sets, or under neither, contribute nothing. For the nearest-option rule itself
($\delta=0$, $q=1$, so $\lambda=1$) the same change raises acceptance by exactly $\Delta$, so on the same answers a
grader's cost is $\lambda$ times the rule's. Table~\ref{tab:variants}'s \emph{predicted} column applies the remark
answer by answer: a correct answer counts at the grader's own acceptance rate through both option sets, a wrong answer
with a single nearest option as in the remark, and any other answer not at all.

\paragraph{Shuffling the displayed order does not hide the rank.} What must be random is which value is the key, not
the order in which the options are shown. So shuffling that order, as BixBench does, does not give $\Gamma=0$. A
sufficient condition is exchangeability. Suppose the $k$ values
$(\text{key},\text{distractor}_1,\dots,\text{distractor}_{k-1})$ are exchangeable, that is, their joint distribution
is unchanged by any reordering. Then the key is equally likely to be any option, so $\Gamma=0$. The converse fails.
Draw any $k$ values, make one of them the key at random, and list the other $k-1$ in increasing order. Every option
is then equally likely to be the key, and $\Gamma=0$. Yet swapping two distractors gives orders that never occur, so
the values are not exchangeable.

\subsection{The correction for guessing}
\label{app:guessing}

The correction for guessing, formula scoring, assumes that guesses fall on the options at random. A grader that
accepts every correct answer and chooses at random on every wrong one would get, after the correction, the tolerance's
score in expectation (\S\ref{sec:guessing}). This subsection shows what the corrected score is made of, why it falls
below the tolerance's on v1.5, and why the correction cannot remove the effect of the options. Table~\ref{tab:formula}
splits the corrected score minus the tolerance by the kind of answer.

Much of the shortfall comes from grades that select no option. In its rights-minus-wrongs form,
$(r-w/(k-1))/n$ for $r$ right and $w$ wrong answers out of $n$, formula scoring takes nothing off for a skipped
question, but BixBench scores a grade that selects no option as wrong. Counting such grades, and empty answers,
as skipped instead moves the corrected score minus the tolerance from $-3.9$ to $+4.0$ for the published gpt-4o runs,
from $-10.8$ to $+1.7$ for gpt-5.1, and from $-6.0$ and $-7.9$ to $+5.4$ and $+5.4$ for gpt-6-luna and

of their wrong answers, in that order. The seven configurations' graders do so on $12.3\%$; counting those grades as
skipped moves their difference to $-3.0$. The other forced gradings of Table~\ref{tab:formula} select no option on fewer
than $3\%$.

The correction rescales a forced score linearly. So it multiplies a difference between option sets, such as $U-P$, by
$k/(k-1)$ instead of removing it. It maps the 2026 system's $64.4\%$ to $52.5\%$, $3.7$ points above that system's
open-ended $48.8\%$. How far $52.5\%$ lies from a tolerance's score depends on where the system's wrong answers fall
among the options, which the correction does not see.

\subsection{The leak}
\label{app:arms}

A key's \emph{rank} is its place among the option values sorted from smallest to largest. A \emph{rank rule} always
selects the option at one rank, without reading the question. In v1.5 the key's rank is far from uniform, so the
options alone reveal where the key tends to sit (\S\ref{sec:leak}). This subsection gives the evidence in full, traces
where the skew came from, and asks whether any model uses it. Table~\ref{tab:survey} gives the survey of nine datasets
of \S\ref{sec:leak} in full. Table~\ref{tab:origin} traces v1.5's skew: besides the distractors v1.5 rewrote
(\S\ref{sec:leak}), it comes from the items v1.5 added, and from the v1.0 items v1.5 kept, whose key was already
second-smallest more often ($40\%$, against $24\%$ of the items it dropped).

\paragraph{No model we tested is shown to use the rank.} BixBench's no-data baseline asks a model to answer each
question without the data, choosing among the options. A model above chance there may recall the answer, or it may
read the key's rank from the options. None of our checks shows a model reading the rank. For BixBench's own runs,
though, we can only bound how much of their margin over chance the rank could explain.

\emph{BixBench's own runs.} Besides its forced-choice runs without the data, BixBench released the same models'
answers without the data in two other conditions: without options, and with an ``insufficient information'' option
added (Table~\ref{tab:arms}). Forced to choose without the data, gpt-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet are $+11.1$
$[+3.8,+18.8]$ and $+9.1$ $[+2.5,+16.4]$ points above chance on v1.5. Yet without options, each gives the right answer
on only $6$ of $205$ questions. A model that drew its whole margin from the rank would be correct far more often where
the key is second-smallest. Both models are instead less accurate there, and they are as far above chance on the
questions whose options are not numbers (Table~\ref{tab:attribution}). The groups of items at each rank are small, so
this comparison bounds the rank's share without excluding it. At the upper ends of the jointly corrected intervals
(Table~\ref{tab:claims}), a preference for the second-smallest option could explain at most $45\%$ of gpt-4o's numeric
margin and $59\%$ of Claude's. Neither model can remember the benchmark: their knowledge cutoffs, October 2023 for
gpt-4o and April 2024 for Claude 3.5 Sonnet, precede BixBench's first release in February 2025 \citep{bixbench}. Both
may still know the biology behind it.

\emph{Our own runs.} gpt-4o 2024-11-20, run again without the data, is at chance when the question is withheld, and it
selects the second-smallest option on $24.1\%$ of v1.5's items. With the question, under BixBench's template,
redrawing every distractor changes its margin by no significant amount on either release; under the chat prompt on
v1.0 it does (Table~\ref{tab:gptnodata}; prompts in Figure~\ref{fig:prompts}). Thirteen open-weight models shown v1.5's options without the question select the
second-smallest on at most $33.3\%$ of their runs, though the key is second-smallest on $51.4\%$ of the items. Showing
eight of them $64$ solved v1.5 items as examples does not measurably change their accuracy, whether the examples' keys
sit at their released ranks or at uniform ones (Figure~\ref{fig:norank}b). Asked for a number without options, six
open-weight models give one within $5\%$ of the key on at most $3.8\%$ of v1.5's numeric items, even counting any
number in the reply. With options, drawing the key's rank uniformly changes the margin of none of the five whose
replies could be read.

\subsection{The cost of hiding the rank}
\label{app:replication}

This subsection gives the details behind \S\ref{sec:cost}. Every answer is graded through three option sets that
share the key: $R$, the released options; $P$, whose distractors are redrawn with the key's rank preserved; and $U$,
whose distractors are redrawn with the key's rank drawn uniformly. The \emph{moved keys} are the keys that $U$ moves
from bracketed (a distractor on each side) to extreme (the smallest or largest option). $U-P$, in percentage points,
is the \emph{cost} of hiding the rank: how many more answers a grading accepts through $U$ than through $P$.

\paragraph{Runs with and without the data.} Of the three agents that write their tool calls as text, one does
better with the data than without it: Qwen2.5-72B. Its submitted number is within $5\%$ of the key on $14.3\%$ of the numeric items with the data,
against $1.0\%$ without it. Yet no grading through options shows its lead over the other two in-text agents with an
interval that excludes zero (Figure~\ref{fig:withdata}b). Table~\ref{tab:withdata} scores each configuration (one
model run one way) with and without the data. Figure~\ref{fig:withdata}a grades the same runs through $R$, $P$ and
$U$. Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} counts the answers that $U$ newly accepts on the moved keys.

\paragraph{The pre-specified test.} We wrote down the test before seeing its data. A plan dated 24 September 2026
fixed the data, the option sets, the groups of keys, the grading rule, the statistics and a pass criterion for each
hypothesis (Table~\ref{tab:predictions}). The plan came before any analysis of the published runs and before the new
runs were made. We committed it to our version history and pushed it to GitHub. GitHub's push record, archived with
the code, dates that push at 16:25 UTC, $15$ minutes before the push of the test's first results. The new runs total
$3{,}280$ question runs, with and without the data: two runs of Qwen3-235B-A22B, and two new seeds of each agent that
writes its tool calls as text, on all $205$ questions.

Two details explain parts of the results. On the published runs, $U$ moves $11$ of the unchanged keys from one
extreme to the other, so their open side changes; on these keys $U-R$ is $+19.1$ points (\S\ref{sec:cost} gives their
share of H2's difference). For Qwen3-235B-A22B, redrawing the distractors is enough to make the rule accept wrong
answers close to the key. On its unchanged keys whose rank $U$ leaves as released, the rule accepts $+6.7$ points
more through $U$ than through $R$, and $+1.7$ more than through
$P$, so most of the gain comes from the redrawing.

\paragraph{Other graders.} A grader's proximity weight $\lambda$ (how closely it follows the nearest option;
\S\ref{sec:theory}) tells which graders bear more of the nearest-option rule's cost, but it predicts the size of that
cost only roughly. Tables~\ref{tab:readers2}, \ref{tab:readersfull} and~\ref{tab:variants} give every grading's
differences. With BixBench's prompt and the notebook, Qwen2.5-72B and Llama-3.3-70B graded a random quarter of the
published runs and every run on a moved key, and gemma-3-27b graded every run of the test. To measure a grader's
$\lambda$, we find the option nearest the number the tolerance reads (ties in $d$ broken by $|a-v|$), and we pool the
grader's selections over every option set.

Over the $46$ gradings of Figure~\ref{fig:cost}a, a grading's share of the rule's cost rises with its $\lambda$ (rank
correlation $0.88$). But the cost that $\lambda$ predicts falls short of the answer-only graders' cost on the
published runs, and exceeds the notebook graders' cost on the new seeds. To check $\lambda$ out of sample, we
estimated it from the unchanged and inward keys alone and used it to predict the moved keys' cost. Over the $42$
gradings that also graded those keys, the prediction errs by $3.9$ points on average, and the observed cost lies
within the prediction's interval for only $18$ of the $42$. Table~\ref{tab:variants}'s \emph{predicted} column uses
$\lambda$ from all keys.

The answer-only graders, which see the question, the options and the answer but not the notebook, follow the nearest
option closely. On the published runs' moved keys they bear $74$ to $90\%$ of the nearest-option rule's cost on the same
answers, with the rule reading the last number, as in Figure~\ref{fig:cost}a. The within-$5\%$ option removes this cost but rejects some correct
answers. Offered it, the answer-only graders select it on $80.1$ to $91.5\%$ of the published runs' wrong answers and
on $5.8$ to $12.3\%$ of their correct answers. Counting the correct answers they map to a wrong option as well, they
reject $6.6$ to $17.9\%$ of the correct answers on the two releases.

\paragraph{Agent accuracy.} The cost of a hidden rank comes almost entirely from wrong answers (Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}),
so it shrinks as agents become more accurate (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}). Per wrong answer on the moved keys, the cost falls with accuracy more
weakly than over all items (\S\ref{sec:cost}): rank correlation $-0.46$, $p=0.050$. The run sets (one configuration's
runs from one seed) are not independent, because several share a model. With each of the eight models counted once,
only the decrease over all items remains ($-0.90$, $p=0.005$).

The share of wrong answers a single grader accepts behaves differently. When one answer-only grader grades $20$ of the
$22$ run sets (the nineteen of Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} and the current agents'), the share of wrong answers it accepts rises with accuracy ($+0.50$ for gemma-3-27b, $+0.53$ for gpt-4o).
The share that each run set's own forced grading accepts does not (\S\ref{sec:withdata}). A more accurate agent's wrong
answers also lie nearest the key more often ($+0.66$, $p=0.001$).

\subsection{Designs of the options}
\label{app:design}

A \emph{design} is one way an author could build an item's options. Every design of \S\ref{sec:design} keeps each
numeric item's key and sets three things. The first is the number of options, $k$. The second is the key's rank:
drawn uniformly from all $k$ ranks (\emph{uniform}); drawn from the $k-2$ middle ranks, so that the key is never
extreme (\emph{middle}); or kept at the key's released rank ($k=4$). The third is the distractors. The generator of
$P$ and $U$ (Appendix~\ref{app:options}) draws the $k-1$ distractors of the redrawn designs. The fixed-spacing designs
place them a fifth or a tenth of the key's value apart, and the designs from agents' errors take them from other agents' numbers for the same question that are more than $5\%$ from the
key and share its sign. A run is graded only through options built without its own model's numbers.

We compare designs on the runs that every design can grade: $4{,}877$ of the $5{,}161$ published runs, and $2{,}070$
of the $2{,}205$ v1.5 runs with the data (the seven configurations', the new seeds', Qwen3-235B-A22B's six runs per
question and gpt-5.1's two).

Under the middle design, the rank rule gains more than Observation~\ref{obs:bracket}'s floor. When the options are
close together, keys written with few digits cannot take every middle rank. So the most frequent middle rank holds
more than its uniform share ($35.4$ against $25\%$ of v1.0's keys at $k=6$), and a rule that always selects it gains
more than the floor (Table~\ref{tab:design}).
Table~\ref{tab:designpairs} sets out what drawing the rank from all $k$ ranks costs.
